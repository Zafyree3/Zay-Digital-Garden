# The Unconscious Casualty

## Nervous System
The nervous system is a complex network of nerve cells and fibres through which signals are relayed between the brain and different parts of the body.

It is divided into the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS). The CNS consists of the brain and spinal cord, while the PNS consists of all the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord.

## Unconsciousness
Unconsciousness occurs when a person is unresponsive to people and the environment. This can lead to impairment in normal awareness, ranging from drowsiness and confusion to total lack of response.

### General First Aid
1. Perform primary and secondary survey.
2. Call for SCDF at 995.
3. If the casualty is breathing normally, check for other injuries.
4. If breathing is absent, begin CPR and use an AED.
5. Do not move the casualty unnecessarily or give anything by mouth.
6. Treat any associated injuries / illness.
7. **Do not** leave the casualty unattended at any time.
8. Monitor the casualty and record the vital signs at regular intervals.

## Head Injury
### General Symptoms
- **Brain injury** — the brain can be bruised or bleed, even if the skull isn't fractured. This can lead to brain swelling, which increases pressure inside the skull and can cause brain damage.
- **Physical symptoms** — headaches, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, loss of balance, and problems with speech.
- **Cognitive symptoms** — memory problems, difficulty thinking, and changes in mood or behaviour.
- **Sensory symptoms** — blurred vision, ringing in the ears, and changes in taste or smell.
- **Other symptoms** — loss of consciousness, seizures, and difficulty walking.

### Types of Head Injuries
- **Concussion** — acceleration and deceleration of the brain against the inside of the skull can irritate the brain and interrupt its function. Function may be interrupted but there is no structural damage to the brain.
- **Cerebral compression** — build-up of pressure on the brain due to accumulation of blood within the skull, swelling of injured brain tissues due to infection, stroke or ruptured blood vessel in the brain, and brain tumours.
- **Skull fractures**

## Spinal Injury
Spinal injuries can be a result of direct impact to the cord itself (accidents, falls from height etc) or indirectly from surrounding bones, tissues and blood vessels (bleeding, fluid accumulation, swelling etc).

## Heat Injury
Prolonged exposure to heat, coupled with strenuous physical activities, can lead to various heat injuries.

### Heat Cramps
A mild form of heat injury that occurs when you sweat too much or do not drink enough water.

**Treatment:**
1. Stop all activity and sit in a cool place.
2. Drink enough water.
3. Seek medical attention if cramps do not subside within 1 hour.
4. Do not resume strenuous activities within 2 hours.

### Heat Exhaustion
Due to dehydration and is more serious.

**Symptoms:**
- Loss of body fluid from sweat
- Headache
- Pale/clammy skin
- Rapid pulse and breathing
- Nausea or vomiting

**Treatment:**
1. Lower body temperature.
2. Move casualty to a cool/shaded place.
3. Remove clothing if possible.
4. Give small sips of water.
5. Monitor and record vital signs.
6. Inform casualty to see a doctor.

### Heat Stroke
Life-threatening due to prolonged exposure to heat.

**Symptoms:**
- Body temperature rises above 40 °C
- Throbbing headache, dizziness, acute discomfort
- Restlessness or confusion
- Hot, flushed and dry skin
- Rapid but weak pulse
- May be unconscious

**Treatment:**
1. Move casualty to a cool/shaded place.
2. Perform primary survey and begin CPR.
3. Remove clothing and lower body temperature by:
   - Sponging
   - Fanning
   - Wrapping in a cool wet sheet
4. Continue cooling until temperature is below 38 °C.
5. Monitor and record vital signs.

## Seizures and Epilepsy
Seizures are episodes of abnormal electrical activity in the brain that result in various symptoms. The prevalence of seizures can be attributed to several factors, including neurological conditions, infections, and other medical issues.

### Seizure Types
- **Focal seizures** — start in one area of the brain and are sometimes called partial seizures.
  - **Simple focal seizures** — affect only a small area of the brain, often causing twitching, unusual tastes or smells, and usually not involving loss of consciousness.
  - **Complex focal seizures** — affect a larger part of the brain, may lead to confusion, repetitive movements, and can involve a loss of awareness.
- **Generalised seizures** — involve both sides of the brain from the beginning and are more likely to result in loss of consciousness or awareness.
  - **Absence seizures** — brief lapses in awareness, often seen as "staring spells".
  - **Tonic-clonic seizures** — the most well-known type, with stiffening and jerking movements, usually involving loss of consciousness.
  - **Atonic seizures** — sudden loss of muscle tone, leading to a "drop" in posture or a fall.
  - **Myoclonic seizures** — quick, sudden jerks of muscles, usually without loss of consciousness.

### Causes of Seizures
1. **Epilepsy** — a chronic neurological disorder where individuals have recurrent, unprovoked seizures.
2. **Infections** — infections like meningitis, encephalitis, or even high fevers in children can lead to seizures.
3. **Trauma** — head injuries, whether recent or past, may trigger seizures.
4. **Stroke** — especially common in older adults, as strokes can disrupt normal brain activity.
5. **Genetic factors** — some genetic conditions may predispose individuals to seizures.
6. **Metabolic imbalances** — issues such as low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia), low sodium, or electrolyte imbalances.
7. **Alcohol and drug use** — substance abuse or sudden withdrawal, especially with alcohol, can lead to seizures.
8. **Brain tumours** — tumours or lesions in the brain can cause seizures, often depending on the location and size.

### First Aid for Seizures
- Clear potentially dangerous items.
- Gently lower the casualty to the ground.
- Do **not** restrain the casualty or place anything into the mouth.
- Document how long the seizure lasted and which body parts were affected.

### After the Seizure
- Reassure the casualty and remove constrictive clothing.
- Monitor and record vital signs, especially level of consciousness.
- Remain with the casualty until they recover from disorientation/confusion.
- Arrange transport to the nearest medical facility if the casualty remains unconscious or if the seizure lasts for more than 5 minutes.
- Call for an ambulance if there is deterioration in the casualty's level of responsiveness or if the casualty does not recover.
- For a child with a high temperature, cool the child before sending to hospital.

### Management and Treatment
- **Medication** — antiepileptic drugs are commonly prescribed to control seizures.
- **Lifestyle adjustments** — avoiding seizure triggers, such as sleep deprivation, stress, or alcohol.
- **Surgery** — in cases where medication does not work, surgery may be an option, especially for focal seizures.
- **Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)** — a device implanted in the body stimulates the vagus nerve to help control seizures.
- **Ketogenic diet** — a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet that has been shown to reduce seizures in some individuals, especially children.

## Fainting or Syncope
Fainting is a sudden, temporary loss of consciousness that usually lasts for a short period and is followed by a quick and spontaneous recovery. Fainting occurs when there is a temporary reduction in blood flow to the brain, often caused by a drop in blood pressure.

### Symptoms Leading Up to Fainting
- Lightheadedness or dizziness
- Nausea
- Sweating
- Blurred or tunnel vision
- Pale skin
- Tingling or weakness

### Types of Fainting
- **Vasovagal syncope** — triggered by a sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure.
- **Situational syncope** — triggered by specific situations or activities, such as:
  - Coughing or sneezing
  - Urination (micturition syncope)
  - Swallowing or defecation
- **Orthostatic hypotension** — a sudden drop in blood pressure that occurs when standing up quickly, which can reduce blood flow to the brain.
- **Cardiac syncope** — caused by heart-related issues, like arrhythmias, heart valve disease, or structural abnormalities; this type of fainting is often more serious.
- **Neurological conditions** — conditions like epilepsy, migraines, or transient ischaemic attacks (TIAs) can sometimes cause fainting episodes.
- **Hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar)** — often seen in people with diabetes or those who haven't eaten for a long time; low blood sugar can lead to fainting.

### Treatment
1. Perform primary and secondary survey.
   - If the casualty is breathing normally, check for injuries before placing on a flat surface with legs elevated.
2. Treat any serious injuries.
3. Monitor and record the vital signs.
4. Advise the casualty to seek medical attention.

### Fainting Questionnaire
- Are you feeling nauseated or dizzy?
- Are you short of breath?
- Have you been taking a new medication?
- Have you been eating and sleeping well?
- How often has this occurred or how often/long have you been feeling faint?

## Diabetic Emergencies
Diabetes is a chronic disease that occurs either when the pancreas does not produce enough insulin or when the body cannot effectively use the insulin it produces. Insulin is a hormone that regulates blood glucose. Uncontrolled diabetes may lead to an emergency where blood sugar levels become too high or too low.

### Hypoglycaemia (Low Blood Sugar)
- Sudden onset
- Pale skin
- Sudden hunger
- Profuse sweating
- Dizziness
- Shallow breathing
- Tremors
- Disorientation
- Coma

### Hyperglycaemia (High Blood Sugar)
- Gradual onset
- Dry skin
- Extreme thirst
- Deep breathing
- Fruity breath
- Very frequent urination
- Drowsiness

### First Aid for Diabetic Emergencies
**If hyperglycaemia:**
- Activate EMS and say you suspect hyperglycaemia.
- Casualty may have a medical bracelet or card to alert on the condition.
- While waiting, check breathing, pulse, and response; perform CPR/AED if needed.

**If hypoglycaemia:**
- Activate EMS and say you suspect hypoglycaemia.
- Assist the casualty to sit down and give something sweet.
- If a blood glucose test kit is available, assist in checking their blood sugar level.
- Remain with the casualty until they feel better.
- Keep monitoring breathing and response.
- If the casualty is not fully alert, avoid giving something to eat or drink as choking may occur.

## Stroke
Stroke is a significant health concern in Singapore, with it being one of the leading causes of death and disability. A stroke occurs when blood flow to a part of the brain is disrupted, either due to a blockage (ischaemic stroke) or a burst blood vessel (haemorrhagic stroke). This interruption prevents oxygen and nutrients from reaching brain tissue, leading to brain cell death. Stroke requires immediate medical attention to minimize damage and improve outcomes.

### Types of Stroke
1. **Ischaemic stroke** — the most common type, caused by a blockage in an artery supplying blood to the brain, often due to blood clots or atherosclerosis (plaque buildup).
2. **Haemorrhagic stroke** — occurs when a blood vessel in the brain bursts, causing bleeding into or around the brain. This can be due to factors like high blood pressure, aneurysms, or blood vessel abnormalities.
3. **Transient ischaemic attack (TIA)** — often called a "mini-stroke", TIA occurs when blood flow to the brain is temporarily blocked. Symptoms are similar to those of a stroke but usually last only a few minutes to an hour. TIAs are a warning sign of a possible future stroke.

### Risk Factors
- **Hypertension** — high blood pressure is a major risk factor for stroke.
- **Diabetes** — people with diabetes are at a higher risk due to damage to blood vessels.
- **High cholesterol** — increases the risk of plaque buildup in arteries.
- **Smoking** — increases blood clot formation and damages blood vessels.
- **Sedentary lifestyle** — lack of physical activity can lead to conditions that increase stroke risk.
- **Diet** — high salt, sugar, and fat intake contribute to high blood pressure, diabetes, and high cholesterol.
- **Obesity** — excess weight increases the risk of diabetes, hypertension, and heart disease.
- **Family history** — people with a family history of stroke may have a higher risk.

### Signs and Symptoms
**Ischaemic:**
- Confusion, trouble speaking or understanding
- Trouble seeing in one or both eyes
- Trouble walking / unsteady gait
- Dizziness
- Tension-like headache
- Nausea or vomiting

**Haemorrhagic:**
- Severe headache at the back of the head
- Trouble speaking or understanding speech
- Vision changes
- Loss of coordination or balance
- Loss of consciousness may occur

**Both:**
- Numbness or weakness in the face or limb, often on one side
- Confusion
- Seizures

### Management and Treatment
- **Medication** — for ischaemic stroke, clot-busting medications (thrombolytics) can help dissolve the clot if administered quickly. Other medications include antiplatelet agents and anticoagulants to prevent clot formation.
- **Surgery** — in some cases, surgical procedures are necessary to repair damaged blood vessels or remove blood clots.
- **Rehabilitation** — post-stroke rehabilitation, including physical, occupational, and speech therapy, is crucial to help regain function and adapt to any lasting impairments.

### Prevention
- **Monitor and control blood pressure** — regular check-ups and medication, if necessary, are key for people with high blood pressure.
- **Exercise regularly** — physical activity helps maintain a healthy weight, reduce blood pressure, and improve cardiovascular health.
- **Maintain a healthy diet** — a balanced diet low in salt, sugar, and unhealthy fats can reduce stroke risk.
- **Quit smoking** — avoid smoking and excessive alcohol consumption to improve vascular health.
- **Manage chronic conditions** — properly managing diabetes, high cholesterol, and other health conditions reduces stroke risk.

## Recovery Position
The recovery position is used in the management of a casualty who is unresponsive but is breathing normally.

When an unresponsive casualty is lying supine (facing up) and breathing normally, their airway may be obstructed by the tongue, mucus and/or vomit.

These problems may be prevented when the casualty is placed in the recovery position. This position keeps the airway open as fluid can drain easily from the mouth. If there is no evidence of trauma, place the casualty on their side to keep the airway open.

> [!note]+ Note
> - For cardiac arrest cases, the casualty should be maintained in a supine position as there is a possibility of re-occurrence.
> - Do not turn the casualty to recovery position if you suspect neck or spinal injury.

### Steps
1. Move the casualty's arm that is closest to you into a right angle position to their body, with their elbow bent and their hand at the level of their head.
2. Gently take hold of their other hand (palm to palm). Now lift this hand across the person's body and position the back of their hand against their opposite cheek (for example, the back of their right hand should be touching their left cheek). Continue to hold your hand in theirs in this position to guide and support their head as you roll them.
3. Use your free hand to reach down to the person's knee that is furthest from you. Lift this knee up such that the leg is bent with the foot resting flat on the floor. Pull the bent knee towards you and roll the casualty onto their side. The leg you have just pulled over should be left in a bent position to keep the person balanced on their side.
4. Gently lift up the casualty's chin to slightly tilt their head back in order to open up the casualty's airway. If you notice an obstruction in the airway, attempt to remove it if it can be done safely. Do not leave the person alone and continue to monitor their breathing until medical help arrives.
