# Burn Injuries

## The Integumentary System
The integumentary system is the body system that includes the skin, hair, nails, glands, and various receptors. It serves as the body's first line of defense against environmental hazards, helps regulate temperature, and allows for sensory perception. The integumentary system plays a crucial role in protecting internal organs, supporting overall health, and maintaining homeostasis.

### Importance
The integumentary system plays a critical role in health and survival by serving as the body's primary barrier against the external environment. It is involved in sensory perception, thermoregulation, and the excretion of waste. Additionally, it aids in vitamin D synthesis, which is essential for bone health. Proper care and maintenance of the skin and other components are vital for overall health and well-being.

### Components
- **Skin** — the largest organ in the body, which has several layers and functions.
- **Hair** — grows from follicles in the dermis and serves to protect and insulate the body. Hair on the scalp protects against UV rays, while eyelashes, eyebrows, and nose hairs protect sensitive areas from dust and particles.
- **Nails** — made of keratin, nails protect the tips of fingers and toes from injury. They also assist in picking up small objects and enhance tactile sensation.
- **Glands** — the skin contains several types of glands with distinct functions:
  - **Sebaceous glands** — produce sebum, an oily substance that lubricates and waterproofs the skin and hair.
  - **Sweat glands** — produce sweat, which helps cool the body and remove waste.
  - **Ceruminous glands** — found in the ear canal, they produce earwax (cerumen), which protects the ear from dust and microorganisms.
- **Sensory receptors** — the skin contains various receptors that detect touch, temperature, pain, and pressure, allowing us to respond to environmental changes.

### Functions
- **Protection** — acts as a physical barrier, protecting the body from pathogens, UV radiation, chemical exposure, and physical injury.
- **Temperature regulation** — sweat glands help cool the body by releasing sweat, which evaporates to lower body temperature. Blood vessels in the skin can dilate to release heat or constrict to retain heat.
- **Sensation** — sensory receptors in the skin allow us to perceive touch, pressure, pain, and temperature, helping us react to the environment and avoid injury.
- **Excretion** — sweat glands help remove waste products such as urea, salts, and toxins through perspiration.
- **Vitamin D synthesis** — when exposed to sunlight, the skin produces vitamin D, essential for calcium absorption and bone health.
- **Water barrier** — the epidermis prevents excessive water loss from the body and protects against water absorption, maintaining proper hydration and fluid balance.

## Skin
### Skin Layers
- **Epidermis** — the outermost layer of skin, which provides a waterproof barrier and creates skin tone. It consists of dead skin cells that are constantly shed and replaced.
- **Dermis** — the middle layer, containing connective tissue, blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, and glands. It provides strength and elasticity.
- **Hypodermis (subcutaneous layer)** — the deepest layer, primarily composed of fat and connective tissue. It provides insulation, cushioning, and anchors the skin to underlying tissues.

## Burns
Burns are injuries to the skin and underlying tissues caused by exposure to heat, chemicals, electricity, radiation, or friction. They damage the skin's structure and disrupt its ability to protect the body, regulate temperature, and prevent fluid loss. Burns vary in severity, from minor injuries that affect only the outer layer of the skin to severe injuries that penetrate deeper tissues and can be life-threatening.

The severity of burns is assessed based on several key factors, including the cause of the burn, depth of the burn, total body surface area (TBSA) affected, the location of the burn, and the age and health of the patient. Proper assessment helps determine the appropriate treatment and the potential for complications.

### Causes of Burns
- **Thermal burns** — caused by direct contact with heat sources such as flames, hot liquids (scalds), hot objects, or steam.
- **Chemical burns** — result from contact with acidic or alkaline substances, like household cleaners, industrial chemicals, or bleach. These can continue to damage tissues until thoroughly washed off.
- **Electrical burns** — caused by contact with electrical currents, which can cause deep tissue damage along the path of the electricity, even if the skin appears only slightly burned.
- **Radiation burns** — result from prolonged exposure to UV radiation (e.g. sunburn) or other radiation sources like X-rays.
- **Friction burns** — caused by skin rubbing against a rough surface, such as carpet or road surfaces, leading to both abrasion and thermal injury.

### Depth of Burns
Burns are classified by depth, which reflects the extent of skin and tissue damage:
- **First-degree burns (superficial)** — affect only the outer layer of the skin, the epidermis.
  - Symptoms: redness, mild swelling, pain, no blisters.
  - Example: sunburn.
- **Second-degree burns (partial-thickness)** — affect both the epidermis and part of the dermis layer.
  - Symptoms: red, swollen skin with blisters and pain; the area may appear wet or shiny.
  - Example: burns from scalding or brief contact with a hot object.
- **Third-degree burns (full-thickness)** — extend through the epidermis and dermis, affecting deeper tissues.
  - Symptoms: the skin may appear white, leathery, or charred. These burns often destroy nerve endings, so they may be painless initially.
  - Example: burns from prolonged exposure to flames, hot surfaces, or chemicals.

### Total Body Surface Area (TBSA)
- **Rule of Nines** — a quick and widely used method, particularly in adults. The body is divided into sections, each representing approximately 9% (or a multiple of 9%) of the body surface area:
  - Head and neck: 9%
  - Each arm: 9% (4.5% for the front and 4.5% for the back)
  - Each leg: 18% (9% for the front and 9% for the back)
  - Front of torso: 18%
  - Back of torso: 18%
  - Genital area: 1%
- **Rule of Palms** — a quick method that estimates burn area using the patient's own palm size. The palm (excluding the fingers) represents approximately 1%.
  - Compare the burned area to the size of the patient's palm and count how many "palms" fit over the burned area.
  - Useful for estimating smaller burns or irregularly shaped burns.
  - Can be used for patients of all ages, but generally not recommended for large burns.

### Locations of Burns
The location of the burn can significantly impact its severity and the risk of complications:
- **Face, neck, and chest burns** — these areas can affect breathing if swelling occurs, requiring close monitoring and possibly intubation.
- **Hands, feet, and joints** — burns in these areas can lead to long-term functional impairments and require specialized treatment to preserve movement.
- **Groin and perineum** — burns in these areas are prone to infection due to their location.
- **Circumferential burns** — burns that encircle the chest, arms, or legs can restrict blood flow and cause compartment syndrome, a condition that requires urgent medical intervention.

### Patient Age and Health Status
- **Infants and young children** — they have thinner skin, so burns may penetrate deeper and have more severe effects, even with relatively minor injuries.
- **Older adults** — they may have thinner skin and other health conditions (like heart disease or diabetes) that can complicate burn healing and increase the risk of infection.
- **Pre-existing medical conditions** — conditions such as diabetes, immune disorders, or respiratory illnesses can worsen the effects of a burn and complicate treatment.

### Classification of Burn Severity
Burn severity is often categorized into three levels:
- **Minor burns**
  - First-degree burns covering less than 10% TBSA
  - Second-degree burns covering less than 10% TBSA in adults, or 5% in children/elderly
  - Typically treated on an outpatient basis
- **Moderate burns**
  - Second-degree burns covering 10–20% TBSA in adults
  - Third-degree burns covering less than 10% TBSA
  - Requires hospital admission for observation and treatment
- **Severe (major) burns**
  - Second-degree burns covering more than 20% TBSA in adults, or 10% in children/elderly
  - Third-degree burns covering more than 10% TBSA
  - Burns involving critical areas (face, hands, feet, major joints, genitalia)
  - All fourth-degree burns
  - Requires specialized burn care, often in a burn unit or ICU, and may need surgery, grafting, and intensive rehabilitation

### First Aid for Burns
> [!info]- Key Principles
> - **Stop the burn** — remove the person from the source of injury.
> - **Cool the area** — use cool, running water (10–20 minutes).
> - **Protect the skin** — cover with a clean, non-stick dressing.

1. **Assess the situation**
   - Ensure the area is safe.
   - Protect yourself before helping others.
2. **Cool the burn**
   - Use cool water (not ice).
   - Avoid lotions, butter, or oils.
3. **Remove constricting items**
   - Remove jewelry, belts, or tight clothing near the burn.

> [!warning]- Special Considerations
> - **Blisters** — don't pop them; they protect against infection.
> - **Chemical burns** — brush off dry chemicals, then rinse thoroughly for 20 minutes.
> - **Electrical burns** — turn off the power source; seek immediate medical help.

### When to Seek Help
- Burns larger than the palm of your hand
- Deep, white, or charred burns
- Burns on the face, hands, feet, or genitals
- Electrical or chemical burns

### Prevention
- **Avoid prolonged sun exposure** — use sunscreen and protective clothing to prevent sunburn.
- **Use protective gear** — wear gloves and appropriate clothing when handling chemicals or working with hot tools.
- **Practice electrical safety** — avoid contact with exposed wires, keep electrical appliances away from water, and use caution around high-voltage equipment.
- **Be careful with open flames** — keep hot liquids, pans, and open flames out of reach of children, and avoid leaving flammable items near the stove.
