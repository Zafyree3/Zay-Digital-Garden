# Road Traffic Accident (RTA)

A road traffic accident (RTA), also known as a motor vehicle accident, occurs when one or more vehicles collide with each other, with pedestrians or animals, or with stationary objects such as buildings, road dividers, or light posts.

These may lead to property damage, injuries, or even fatalities.

## Process of First Aid for RTAs (ABCDE)
![rta-abcde-flowchart.jpg](/img/user/Study/First%20Aid%20Course/Images/rta-abcde-flowchart.jpg)

### Airway with C-Spine
- **Look** — without moving the casualty, visibly inspect the neck for any obvious injuries to the neck and spine. Sometimes, major injuries such as significant facial trauma, facial burns, and haemorrhage may cause airway compromise.
- **Listen** — listen to how they sound, i.e. are they able to talk normally, do they have a hoarse voice or stridor?

### Breathing
Look for signs of impaired breathing such as shortness of breath, cyanosis (bluish discoloration of the extremities or lips), and injury or deformity to the chest. Observe for uneven or unequal movement of the chest, which may indicate:
- **Pneumothorax** — occurs when air enters the chest cavity, preventing proper expansion of the lung on the affected side, leading to uneven chest movements.
- **Flail chest** — occurs when multiple ribs located next to each other are fractured in two or more places, leading to the chest wall becoming unstable and interfering with breathing.

### Circulation
Circulation injuries in RTAs may include but are not limited to:
- **External bleeding** — check for open wounds and blood loss. Remember not to remove any penetrating objects.
- **Intra-thoracic bleeding** — check for signs of haemothorax (a condition where blood collects in the hollow area between the lungs and rib cage).
- **Intra-abdominal bleeding** — observe for abdominal distension, bruising, and tenderness.
- **Intra-pelvic bleeding** — carefully assess the pelvis for stability and signs of bleeding such as bruising.
- **Long bone fractures** — check the femur for major bleeding due to the presence of major blood vessels in the area.

Look for signs of haemorrhagic or hypovolemic shock even if there are no obvious signs of bleeding elsewhere.

### Disability
- Assess the casualty's level of consciousness using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) or AVPU scale.
- Assess whether the casualty's pupils are equal and reactive to light (PEARL).

### Exposure
- Expose and examine the casualty for other visible injuries such as deformity, bleeding, or fractures. If you need to move the casualty, adopt a log roll technique.
- Assess the body temperature, which may indicate blood loss or shock.

## Glasgow Coma Scale
### Eye Opening (1–4)
1. No response
2. Opens to pain
3. Opens to sound
4. Opens spontaneously

### Verbal Response (1–5)
1. No response
2. Incomprehensible sounds
3. Inappropriate words
4. Confused
5. Oriented (responds appropriately)

### Motor Response (1–6)
1. No response
2. Extension to pain
3. Flexion to pain
4. Withdraws from pain
5. Localizes to pain
6. Obeys commands

## AVPU
- **Alert** — the casualty is fully awake and responds to questions appropriately.
- **Verbal** — the casualty responds to verbal stimuli (speaking, shouting, or verbal commands).
- **Pain** — the casualty responds to painful stimuli.
- **Unresponsive** — the casualty does not respond to any stimuli (environmental, verbal, or pain).

## First Aid for an RTA
- Ensure safety and get help — protect yourself and others. If there is immediate danger, consider extricating the casualty from the vehicle safely. Get help while doing so, including calling for emergency services if this has not been done.
- Assess the casualty's condition using ABCDE and determine the level of responsiveness.
- Render appropriate first aid treatment, including stabilization and immobilization.
- Arrange for transport to a medical facility.

## Rapid Extrication
**Rapid extrication** is the removal of a person from a vehicle when the situation or condition demands it. This is used only in critical circumstances due to the potential risk of worsening injuries, especially spinal injuries. Primary indications for rapid extrication are:

1. **Unsafe environment**
   - Vehicle instability — if the vehicle is at risk of collapsing, rolling, on unstable ground, or in imminent danger of explosion (e.g. from a fuel leak)
   - Fire, smoke, or hazardous materials — if there is fire, leaking fuel, or hazardous materials that put the casualty and rescuers at risk
2. **Requires immediate assessment and care**
   - The casualty cannot be properly assessed without being removed from the vehicle
   - Life-threatening injuries that cannot be managed within the vehicle, e.g. cardiac arrest, respiratory compromise
3. **Requires immediate transport**
   - Critical trauma requiring advanced care in a controlled environment, e.g. neurological deterioration, multi-trauma, uncontrolled haemorrhage
4. **Inability to access or stabilize vital functions**
   - Rescuers are not able to safely and adequately provide aid due to vehicle constraints
   - The casualty is blocking access to another seriously injured person

### Rapid Extrication Devices
- **Hydraulic rescue tools** — to free casualties from vehicles
- **Halligan bar** — to pry or force a door or window open to gain entry
- **Spring-loaded window punch** — used to break or shatter vehicle glass
- **Kendrick Extrication Device (KED)** — to immobilize and extricate the casualty from confined spaces
- **Spinal boards** — provide rigid support to transport a casualty
- **Skeds or half-skeds** — used to rapidly extricate from narrow spaces
