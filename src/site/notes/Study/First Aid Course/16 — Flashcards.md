# First Aid Course — Flashcards

#flashcards

## 01 — Essential of First Aid

What is the definition of first aid?::Immediate care given to someone who has been injured or suddenly ill, before professional medical help arrives
What must a first aider ensure before anything else?::Their own safety
Where do most medical emergencies occur, and what effect does help have?::Most medical emergencies occur out-of-hospital; when help is rendered, the casualty's chances of recovery significantly increase
Name the five benefits of first aid.::Save lives; reduce recovery time; reduce pain and discomfort; improve confidence during emergencies; create safer communities
What are the four aims of first aid?::Preserve life; prevent the condition from worsening; promote recovery; provide comfort and relief
What type of consent applies when the casualty is unconscious?::Implied consent — consent is required to perform first aid, but implied consent applies if the casualty is unconscious
State three limits of a first aider.::First aiders do not diagnose; they do not administer medications; first aid is only for acute injuries or sudden illness, not complex trauma or emotional/psychological trauma
What four universal precautions minimize cross-contamination?::Cover open wounds with a bandage; use disposable gloves; practice good hygiene; use PPE (personal protective equipment)
Which item in the first aid kit has high versatility?::The triangular bandage
What injuries does each mechanism suggest: fall from height, ejection from a vehicle, sudden deceleration?::Fall from height suggests multiple fractures; ejection from a vehicle suggests multiple injuries; sudden deceleration suggests blunt trauma
What are the six steps of the primary survey, in order?::Danger; Response; Shout for help; AED; Breathing; CPR
For trained healthcare professionals, what sequence replaces the basic primary survey?::Rescue scene evaluation; Airway and C-spine; Breathing; Circulation; Disability (neurological); Exposure and Environment
When is the secondary survey performed, and what happens to the information collected?::It is performed after the primary survey when the casualty's life is not in immediate danger, and the information is passed on to the paramedics taking over care
What does SAMPLE stand for in history taking?::Signs and symptoms; Allergies; Medication; Past history; Last meal; Events/Environment
What does DOTAS stand for in the head-to-toe examination?::Deformity; Open wounds; Tenderness; Abnormal sensation; Swelling
Name the vital signs checked in the secondary survey.::Pulse rate; breathing rate; skin color (flushed, cyanosis, paleness, jaundice, bruising, clammy); body temperature; blood pressure; blood oxygen saturation

## 02 — The Unconscious Casualty

How is the nervous system divided?::Into the central nervous system (CNS), made of the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS), made of all nerves outside the brain and spinal cord
What is unconsciousness?::Being unresponsive to people and the environment, with impairment of normal awareness ranging from drowsiness and confusion to total lack of response
What is the emergency number for SCDF in Singapore?::995
Give the general first aid steps for an unconscious casualty.::Perform primary and secondary survey; call SCDF at 995; if breathing normally check for other injuries, but if breathing is absent begin CPR and use an AED; do not move the casualty unnecessarily or give anything by mouth; treat associated injuries; never leave the casualty unattended; monitor and record vital signs at regular intervals
What is a concussion?::Acceleration and deceleration of the brain against the inside of the skull that irritates the brain and interrupts its function, but causes no structural damage
What is cerebral compression?::A build-up of pressure on the brain due to accumulation of blood within the skull, swelling of injured brain tissue, infection, stroke, ruptured blood vessel, or brain tumours
In heat cramps, when should medical attention be sought and when can strenuous activity resume?::Seek medical attention if cramps do not subside within 1 hour; do not resume strenuous activities within 2 hours
What are the symptoms of heat exhaustion?::Loss of body fluid from sweat, headache, pale/clammy skin, rapid pulse and breathing, nausea or vomiting — due to dehydration and more serious than heat cramps
What temperature threshold defines heat stroke, and how long must cooling continue?::Body temperature rises above 40 °C; continue cooling (sponging, fanning, wrapping in a cool wet sheet) until the temperature is below 38 °C
Describe the generalised seizure types.::Absence seizures — brief lapses in awareness, seen as staring spells; tonic-clonic seizures — stiffening and jerking movements, usually with loss of consciousness; atonic seizures — sudden loss of muscle tone causing a drop or fall; myoclonic seizures — quick sudden muscle jerks, usually without loss of consciousness
How do focal seizures differ from generalised seizures?::Focal seizures start in one area of the brain (simple ones cause twitching or unusual tastes/smells without loss of consciousness; complex ones cause confusion or repetitive movements with possible loss of awareness); generalised seizures involve both sides of the brain from the start and more often cause loss of consciousness
List the causes of seizures.::Epilepsy; infections such as meningitis, encephalitis or high fevers in children; head trauma; stroke; genetic factors; metabolic imbalances such as low blood sugar, low sodium or electrolyte imbalances; alcohol and drug use or sudden withdrawal; brain tumours
What must you NOT do during a seizure, and what should you record?::Do not restrain the casualty and do not place anything into the mouth; gently lower them to the ground, clear dangerous items, and document how long the seizure lasted and which body parts were affected
After a seizure, when must you arrange transport to a medical facility?::If the casualty remains unconscious or the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes; also call an ambulance if responsiveness deteriorates or the casualty does not recover
Name the management and treatment options for seizures.::Antiepileptic medication; lifestyle adjustments avoiding triggers such as sleep deprivation, stress or alcohol; surgery (especially for focal seizures); vagus nerve stimulation (VNS); and the ketogenic diet — a high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet that reduces seizures, especially in children
What is fainting, and name its main types.::A sudden, temporary loss of consciousness with quick spontaneous recovery, caused by a temporary reduction in blood flow to the brain, often from a drop in blood pressure; types are vasovagal, situational (coughing/sneezing, urination, swallowing or defecation), orthostatic hypotension, cardiac, neurological, and hypoglycaemia
How do the signs of hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia differ?::Hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar) has sudden onset with pale skin, sudden hunger, profuse sweating, dizziness, shallow breathing, tremors, disorientation and coma; hyperglycaemia (high blood sugar) has gradual onset with dry skin, extreme thirst, deep breathing, fruity breath, very frequent urination and drowsiness
In a diabetic emergency with suspected hypoglycaemia, what first aid is given and what must be avoided?::Activate EMS stating suspected hypoglycaemia, assist the casualty to sit down and give something sweet, check blood sugar if a test kit is available, remain until they feel better and keep monitoring; if not fully alert, avoid giving food or drink as choking may occur
What are the three types of stroke and their causes?::Ischaemic stroke — a blockage in an artery supplying the brain, the most common type; haemorrhagic stroke — a burst blood vessel causing bleeding in or around the brain; transient ischaemic attack (TIA) — a temporary blockage, a mini-stroke whose symptoms last minutes to an hour and warn of a possible future stroke
Which medication can treat ischaemic stroke if given quickly, and what is the main prevention?::Clot-busting medications (thrombolytics); prevention focuses on monitoring/controlling blood pressure, regular exercise, a healthy diet low in salt, sugar and unhealthy fats, quitting smoking, and managing chronic conditions
What is the recovery position used for, and which casualties should NOT be placed in it?::For a casualty who is unresponsive but breathing normally, to keep the airway open so fluid drains from the mouth; do not use it for cardiac arrest (keep supine due to possible re-occurrence) or if neck or spinal injury is suspected

## 03 — Respiratory Problems

What is the main job of the respiratory system?::To move fresh air into the body and remove waste gases; oxygen moves into the bloodstream and is exchanged at each cell for the waste gas carbon dioxide
Describe the mechanism of inhalation.::Diaphragm contraction, intercostal muscle action, increase in thoracic volume, decrease in intrapulmonary pressure, and air flows into the lungs
Describe the mechanism of exhalation.::Diaphragm relaxation, intercostal muscle relaxation, decrease in thoracic volume, and air flows out of the lungs
Where and how is breathing regulated?::By the respiratory centre in the brainstem; chemoreceptors in the brainstem and blood vessels detect carbon dioxide, oxygen and blood pH, and if carbon dioxide rises the brain increases breathing rate and depth; breathing is also under some voluntary control
What is asthma and what are its common triggers?::A chronic condition where airways become inflamed and narrowed; triggers include dust mites, medicine, mold, pollen, pets, cold air, smoking, exercise, pollution and stress
What are the signs and symptoms of an asthma attack, and the first aid?::Wheezing, cough, shortness of breath, difficulty breathing, pale clammy skin, dyspnoea and chest tightness; assist the casualty to use a quick-relief inhaler if available, seek medical assistance, and call emergency services if the person does not improve
Give the steps for using a metered-dose inhaler (MDI) with a spacer.::Prime by shaking for 10 seconds; remove the cap and spray 2 or 4 puffs into the air; connect the MDI to the spacer holding it upright; tilt the head back slightly; place the mouthpiece between teeth and lips, or the face mask over nose and mouth to form a seal; actuate once; take 5–10 deep slow breaths; wait 30 seconds before the next actuation
How does using an MDI without a spacer differ, and what does the spacer's flap movement indicate?::The steps are the same minus connecting the spacer, ending with mouthpiece between teeth and lips; the flap movement on the spacer indicates the number of breaths taken
What is hyperventilation, its symptoms, and its treatment?::Rapid or deep breathing, often from anxiety or panic, that causes excessive release of carbon dioxide; symptoms are rapid breathing, tingling in hands or feet, lightheadedness and sometimes chest pain; treatment is encouraging slow deep breathing to rebalance CO2 and seeking medical help if symptoms persist
What are the symptoms and treatment of fumes inhalation?::Headache, confusion, breathlessness, coughing, wheezing, skin discoloration, restlessness or excitement, and unconsciousness; ensure the area is safe to enter — do not enter without proper equipment — bring the casualty to a clear open space and assess for ABC
What is anaphylaxis, its triggers, and its symptoms?::A severe, life-threatening allergic reaction causing swelling of the airways; triggered by allergens such as food, insect stings or medications; symptoms include swelling of the face, lips and throat, difficulty breathing, hives, and a rapid drop in blood pressure
What is the first aid treatment for anaphylaxis?::Administer an epinephrine injection if available, call emergency services immediately, and keep the person calm and in a comfortable position
Give the EpiPen steps and the key numbers.::Form a fist and pull the BLUE safety cap off; position the ORANGE end about 10 cm from the outer mid-thigh (avoiding seams and pockets); swing and jab the ORANGE tip into the thigh at a 90 degree angle and hold for 10 seconds; remove and massage the injection site for 10 seconds
What is pneumothorax, its symptoms, and its treatment?::Air leaks into the space between the lung and chest wall, causing the lung to collapse, from trauma, medical conditions or spontaneously; symptoms are sudden sharp chest pain, shortness of breath and sometimes a bluish skin tint; treatment is seeking medical help, positioning the person comfortably, and covering the wound with an occlusive dressing
Which groups are at risk from flu-related respiratory distress and what are its symptoms?::Young children, the elderly and those with pre-existing conditions; symptoms are severe shortness of breath, high fever, chest pain and confusion — immediate medical evaluation is needed as complications like pneumonia can be life-threatening
What is croup, and how is it managed?::A viral infection causing inflammation of the upper airway, mainly in young children, with a barking cough, noisy breathing (stridor), fever and hoarse voice; keep the child calm and provide cool or moist air such as from a humidifier, and seek medical help if breathing becomes laboured or the child is distressed

## 04 — Circulatory Injuries

What are the main components and functions of the circulatory system?::Components are the heart, blood vessels and blood; functions are transport of oxygen and nutrients, removal of waste products, hormone distribution, temperature regulation, and immune response
Describe blood flow through the four heart chambers.::Right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the body and pumps it to the right ventricle; right ventricle pumps it to the lungs via the pulmonary arteries; left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the left ventricle; left ventricle pumps it to the body through the aorta, the largest artery
What are the three types of blood vessels and their roles?::Arteries carry oxygen-rich blood away from the heart (branching into arterioles, with the aorta the main artery); capillaries are tiny vessels where oxygen, nutrients and waste are exchanged with tissues; veins carry deoxygenated blood back to the heart, with the superior and inferior vena cava the main veins
What are the four components of blood and their roles?::Red blood cells contain haemoglobin which binds and transports oxygen; white blood cells fight infection and disease; platelets help blood clotting to prevent bleeding; plasma is the liquid component carrying nutrients, hormones and waste
What is shock?::A life-threatening condition in which organs and tissues do not receive enough blood flow or oxygen, which can lead to organ failure and death if not treated promptly
Match each type of shock to its cause.::Hypovolemic — significant loss of blood or body fluids from haemorrhage, dehydration or burns; cardiogenic — the heart cannot pump effectively due to damage or disease such as heart attack, heart failure or severe arrhythmias; septic — a severe infection spreading through the body causing a drop in blood pressure; anaphylactic — a severe allergic reaction dilating blood vessels and constricting airways; neurogenic — damage to the nervous system such as spinal cord injury causing loss of vascular tone and low blood pressure
Which internal-bleeding signs point to hypovolemic shock, and what are its control measures?::Profuse bleeding, sweating, dizziness, confusion, shallow breathing, plus black stools, abdominal pain and vomiting blood if bleeding is internal; control measures are controlling haemorrhages and fractures, relieving pain, maintaining body heat, monitoring vital signs, laying flat with legs elevated, and seeking emergency care
What distinguishes neurogenic shock and its special control measure?::Signs include fainting, sudden low blood pressure, warm/flushed skin from vasodilation and a slower heart rate; do not move the victim with a suspected spinal injury
What are the body's responses to blood loss of 300–500 ml, 500–1500 ml, 1500–2000 ml and more than 2000 ml?::300–500 ml — little to no effect; 500–1500 ml — increased diastolic blood pressure, slightly increased pulse rate, pale cool clammy skin; 1500–2000 ml — increased pulse rate, shallow rapid breathing, pale cool clammy skin, nausea, drowsiness; more than 2000 ml — pulse undetectable, loss of consciousness, breathing may cease and the heart may stop
What does hospital treatment for shock generally include?::Fluid resuscitation with IV fluids or blood transfusions; oxygen therapy or mechanical ventilation; medications such as vasopressors or inotropes to stabilize blood pressure; and treating the underlying cause (antibiotics for sepsis, epinephrine for anaphylaxis, surgery for trauma)
How are the three types of external bleeding distinguished?::Capillary bleeding is the mildest, oozing slowly from small cuts or scrapes; venous bleeding flows steadily and may appear dark red due to lower oxygen; arterial bleeding is most severe, spurting in rhythm with the heartbeat, bright red and life-threatening
List the signs of significant blood loss.::Rapid pulse, low blood pressure, pale cool clammy skin, weakness or dizziness, and confusion or loss of consciousness in severe cases
Give the first aid steps for external bleeding.::Apply direct pressure with gloved hands and place a clean dressing over the wound as soon as possible; apply a bulky pad extending beyond the wound edges and bandage firmly, adding another bandage on top if bleeding continues; do not disturb pads or bandages once bleeding is controlled
Give the first aid steps for internal bleeding.::Call SCDF at 995; perform primary and secondary survey; lay the casualty down and raise the legs above the level of the heart unless there is a lower-limb fracture, pelvis injury or spinal injury; loosen tight clothing and give nothing by mouth; commence CPR and use an AED if not breathing; reassure and do not leave unattended; monitor and record vital signs
Match each wound type to its key first aid rule.::Abrasions — flush with sterile water once bleeding stops; lacerations — jagged edges, may bleed moderately to heavily; incisions — clean-edged, may hide damage to tendons, nerves or vessels; amputations — control bleeding first, preserve the part with an ice pack but never let it touch ice directly; punctures — do not remove any embedded object, apply pressure around it if bleeding is severe
Give the first aid for nosebleeds, bee stings, snake bites and jellyfish stings.::Nosebleeds — apply pressure at the soft part of the nose for about 10 minutes; bee stings — scrape out the stinger, wash with soap and water, apply ice, and seek immediate care for allergic reactions or multiple stings; snake bites — treat as life-threatening, call SCDF 995, remove constricting accessories, keep the area below heart level, keep the casualty calm and photograph the snake if safe; jellyfish stings — call SCDF 995, pour vinegar or sea water over the site for at least 30 seconds, and immerse in hot water for 10–20 minutes for pain
What is the difference between a dressing and a bandage?::A dressing is a sterile material applied directly to the wound to protect it, absorb fluids and aid healing; a bandage secures the dressing in place, adds support and helps maintain pressure
Name the types of dressings and bandages.::Dressings — gauze, non-adherent, hydrocolloid, foam, transparent film and hydrogel; bandages — roller (crepe and gauze roller), triangular, tubular, elastic (compression) and adhesive
What are the four steps for applying dressings and bandages?::Clean the wound with water or saline; apply a sterile dressing covering the entire wound; secure with a bandage wrapped firmly but not so tightly that blood flow is restricted; check circulation by testing skin color, warmth and sensation
When should a tourniquet be applied, and when should it NOT be used?::Apply for severe life-threatening limb bleeding that direct pressure cannot stop (rhythmic spurting arterial blood, large wounds, amputations), when pressure is impossible, or in mass-casualty situations; do not use for minor bleeding or anything other than limbs, as prolonged use can cause nerve and tissue damage or limb loss
Give the steps for applying a tourniquet.::Tie the broad bandage with a half hitch about 5 cm above the injury; place a stick in place with another half hitch; twist the stick to tighten, stopping once bleeding stops; record the time of application

## 05 — Musculoskeletal Injuries

What makes up the musculoskeletal system, and what are the three muscle types?::Bones, muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments and other connective tissues; muscle types are skeletal (voluntary, attached to bones), smooth (involuntary, in organs and blood vessels) and cardiac (the heart's specialized involuntary muscle)
What connects what: tendons, ligaments and cartilage?::Tendons connect muscles to bones; ligaments connect bones to each other at joints, providing stability and limiting excessive movement; cartilage is a flexible rubbery tissue covering and cushioning the ends of bones at joints
List the functions of the musculoskeletal system.::Movement; support and stability; protection of organs; mineral storage (calcium and phosphorus in bones); blood cell production in bone marrow; and heat production from muscle contraction
Define simple (closed) and compound (open) fractures.::Simple (closed) — the bone breaks but does not puncture the skin, reducing infection risk; compound (open) — the broken bone pierces the skin, creating an open wound with a high risk of infection needing prompt treatment
Match these fracture types to their descriptions: greenstick, hairline, comminuted, transverse, oblique, spiral, compression, avulsion, pathologic.::Greenstick — one side bends and cracks, other side intact, common in children; hairline (stress) — thin crack from repetitive stress or overuse; comminuted — bone broken into multiple fragments from high-impact trauma; transverse — straight-line break across the bone from a direct blow; oblique — break at an angle from a sharp angled blow; spiral — fracture spirals around the bone from twisting force; compression — bone crushed, often in vertebrae with osteoporosis; avulsion — a small piece pulled off by a tendon or ligament, seen in sports; pathologic — fracture in a bone weakened by disease such as osteoporosis, cancer or infection
What are the symptoms of a fracture?::Severe localized pain, swelling and bruising, deformity, loss of function, and crepitus — a grating or grinding sound from bone fragments rubbing together
What are the complications of fractures?::Infection (especially open fractures), nonunion or malunion, nerve or blood vessel damage, and compartment syndrome — swelling that increases pressure and reduces blood flow, a medical emergency
Give the first aid steps for fractures.::Cover open fractures with a dressing and apply pressure around the area, not on the bone; call SCDF at 995 and, if there is no immediate danger, do not move or splint the limb but wait for paramedics; reassure the casualty not to move; immobilize/splint and treat for associated injuries or shock; do not move the casualty until the part is secured; check for signs of impaired circulation; do not allow anything to eat or drink
Which joint is the most commonly dislocated and in which direction, and which dislocations carry special risks?::The shoulder is most commonly dislocated, with anterior (forward) most common; elbow dislocations are common in children; hip dislocations can restrict the blood supply to the hip; knee dislocations can damage arteries and nerves and often require surgery
What are the symptoms and complications of dislocations?::Severe pain worsening with movement, visible deformity, swelling and bruising, limited mobility, and numbness or tingling from nerve pressure; complications are joint instability, nerve or blood vessel damage, osteoarthritis over time, and avascular necrosis — death of bone tissue from lack of blood supply, as in hip dislocations
What are the functions and types of splints?::Functions — immobilization, pain reduction, protection and swelling control; types — rigid (wood, plastic, metal), soft (foam or padded fabric), air (inflatable), traction (for femur fractures, applying pulling force) and anatomical (using another body part)
Which joints must be kept motionless when splinting each fracture site?::Lower arm — wrist and elbow; upper arm — shoulder and elbow; lower leg — knee and ankle; upper leg — hip and knee
State the principles of splinting.::Identify the fracture site and stop bleeding with bandages without pressing on the painful deformed site; never push bone ends that protrude through skin back in; keep the bone and the joints above and below motionless; tie the splint firmly then check circulation; if the limb is bent with a sharp protruding bone end, splint it as you find it
Give the steps for applying a splint.::Prepare a rigid material with padding; keep the limb in a natural position without realigning displaced bones; place soft padding between splint and skin; position the splint extending beyond the injury on both sides; secure firmly but not so tight as to restrict blood flow; check skin below for warmth, color and sensation
What are sprains, strains, contusions and tendinitis, and their common sites?::Sprains injure a ligament (common in ankle, wrist, knee); strains injure a muscle or tendon from overstretching or tearing (common in back, hamstrings, shoulder); contusions are bruises from direct impact; tendinitis is tendon inflammation from repetitive motion (common at elbow/tennis elbow, shoulder, wrist, Achilles)
What does P.R.I.C.E. stand for in soft tissue injury first aid?::Protect the area from further damage; Rest by avoiding worsening activities; Ice for 15–20 minutes every few hours for the first 48 hours; Compression with a bandage to control swelling; Elevation of the area above heart level to reduce swelling

## 06 — Burn Injuries

What is the integumentary system?::The body system made up of the skin, hair, nails, glands and various receptors; it is the body's first line of defence against environmental hazards, helps regulate temperature and allows sensory perception.
What are the main functions of the skin?::Protection against pathogens, UV radiation, chemicals and injury; temperature regulation; sensation; excretion of waste (urea, salts, toxins) via sweat; vitamin D synthesis for calcium absorption and bone health; and acting as a water barrier against excessive fluid loss.
Which skin components protect against UV rays, dust and particles?::Hair on the scalp protects against UV rays, while eyelashes, eyebrows and nose hairs protect sensitive areas from dust and particles.
What do the sebaceous, sweat and ceruminous glands produce?::Sebaceous glands produce sebum (an oily substance that lubricates and waterproofs skin and hair); sweat glands produce sweat (cools the body and removes waste); ceruminous glands in the ear canal produce earwax (cerumen), which protects the ear from dust and microorganisms.
Name the three skin layers and describe each.::Epidermis — the outermost layer giving a waterproof barrier and skin tone, made of dead skin cells constantly shed and replaced; Dermis — the middle layer with connective tissue, blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands, providing strength and elasticity; Hypodermis (subcutaneous layer) — the deepest layer of fat and connective tissue providing insulation, cushioning and anchoring to underlying tissues.
What are the five causes of burns?::Thermal (flames, hot liquids/scalds, hot objects, steam), chemical (acids or alkalis like cleaners and bleach — they keep damaging tissue until thoroughly washed off), electrical (deep tissue damage along the current path even if the skin looks only slightly burned), radiation (e.g. sunburn from UV or X-rays) and friction (skin rubbing against rough surfaces like carpet or roads).
Describe a first-degree burn.::A superficial burn affecting only the epidermis; symptoms are redness, mild swelling and pain with no blisters; example — sunburn.
Describe a second-degree burn.::A partial-thickness burn affecting the epidermis and part of the dermis; symptoms are red, swollen skin with blisters and pain, and the area may look wet or shiny; example — scalding or brief contact with a hot object.
Describe a third-degree burn.::A full-thickness burn extending through the epidermis and dermis into deeper tissues; skin may look white, leathery or charred, and because nerve endings are often destroyed it may be painless initially; example — prolonged exposure to flames, hot surfaces or chemicals.
Give the Rule of Nines percentages for adults.::Head and neck 9%; each arm 9%; each leg 18%; front of torso 18%; back of torso 18%; genital area 1%.
What is the Rule of Palms and when is it used?::The patient's own palm excluding the fingers represents about 1% of body surface area; count how many palms fit over the burned area. It suits smaller or irregularly shaped burns, works for all ages, but is not recommended for large burns.
Why are burn locations like the face/neck/chest, hands/feet/joints and groin/perineum especially dangerous?::Face, neck and chest burns can affect breathing if swelling occurs (close monitoring and possibly intubation needed); hands, feet and joints risk long-term functional impairment; groin and perineum burns are prone to infection.
What is a circumferential burn and its main danger?::A burn that encircles the chest, arms or legs; it can restrict blood flow and cause compartment syndrome, which requires urgent medical intervention.
Why do infants and older adults suffer more severe burn effects?::Infants and young children have thinner skin, so burns penetrate deeper even from minor injuries; older adults have thinner skin plus conditions like heart disease or diabetes that complicate healing and increase infection risk.
Classify minor, moderate and severe burns by their numbers.::Minor — first-degree under 10% TBSA, or second-degree under 10% TBSA in adults (5% in children/elderly), treated as an outpatient; Moderate — second-degree covering 10–20% TBSA in adults, or third-degree under 10% TBSA, requires hospital admission; Severe — second-degree over 20% TBSA in adults (10% in children/elderly), third-degree over 10% TBSA, burns of critical areas (face, hands, feet, major joints, genitalia), and all fourth-degree burns, needing burn unit or ICU care.
What are the three key first aid principles for burns?::Stop the burn (remove the person from the source), cool the area with cool running water for 10–20 minutes (never ice, and no lotions, butter or oils), and protect the skin with a clean, non-stick dressing; also remove constricting items like jewellery, belts and tight clothing near the burn.
How do you manage burn blisters and chemical or electrical burns?::Never pop blisters — they protect against infection; for chemical burns brush off dry chemicals first then rinse thoroughly for 20 minutes; for electrical burns turn off the power source and seek immediate medical help.
When should you seek medical help for a burn?::If it is larger than the palm of the hand, deep/white/charred, on the face, hands, feet or genitals, or if it is an electrical or chemical burn.

## 07 — Other First Aid Knowledge

How does the eye capture and process light?::The cornea and lens focus light onto the retina; photoreceptors in the retina detect light and convert it into electrical signals; the optic nerve transmits the signals to the brain; and the visual cortex interprets them into images.
What are the signs and symptoms of a penetrating eye injury?::Severe eye pain, decreased or blurred vision, a visible foreign body or wound, blood within the eye (hyphema), tears in the iris, lens or cornea, leakage of clear or bloody fluid, and swelling and bruising around the eye.
What is the first aid for a penetrating eye injury?::Protect the injured eye with an eye pad or shield; cover the uninjured eye with a patch to minimise eye movement; call EMS at 995; never remove any object stuck in the eye; do not apply too much pressure to the uninjured eye; if the object is too large to cover, apply penetrating-object techniques.
How do acids and alkalis differ in their effect on the eye?::Acids (e.g. sulfuric acid in car batteries, hydrochloric acid, vinegar) cause immediate pain and burning and coagulate proteins on the eye surface, sometimes limiting the depth of damage; alkalis (e.g. ammonia, sodium hydroxide/lye, lime from cement) penetrate more quickly and deeply, saponifying fats in cell membranes and causing more severe damage.
What is the first aid for a chemical injury to the eye?::Irrigate immediately with copious clean water or saline, flushing from the nose side outwards for at least 15–20 minutes (longer for severe cases, especially alkalis); have the casualty roll their eyeball during flushing; remove contact lenses; do not rub the eye; then seek immediate medical attention.
How do you manage an embedded foreign object in the eye?::Protect the injured eye with an eye pad or shield so the object is not driven deeper; cover the uninjured eye to stop movement; call SCDF at 995; do not let the casualty rub the eye; never attempt to remove the object; never use dry cotton or instruments such as tweezers on the eye.
What complications can an untreated foreign object in the eye cause?::Corneal abrasions (painful scratches that raise infection risk), infections such as conjunctivitis or keratitis (especially with organic material), scarring that impairs vision, and permanent vision impairment.
Name the types of cuts (lacerations) to the eye and eyelids.::Superficial eyelid lacerations (small surface cuts); full-thickness eyelid lacerations (through the whole eyelid, possibly involving eye muscles); corneal lacerations (cut on the cornea, serious as they can damage vision); and scleral lacerations (cut in the white of the eye, often a deeper injury possibly involving the retina).
What first aid is given for cuts to the eye or eyelids?::Lightly cover the eye with a clean, sterile bandage or eye shield without pressing on the wound; seek immediate medical attention; do not rub or press the eye; and if there is a deep cut on the eye itself, avoid rinsing as it could worsen the injury.
Distinguish anterior and posterior nosebleeds.::Anterior nosebleeds are the most common, arising from vessels in the front nasal septum; they are usually mild and easy to control. Posterior nosebleeds come from deeper vessels, are less common but more serious, harder to control and may need medical intervention.
What is the first aid for a nosebleed?::Sit the casualty with the head slightly forward; pinch the fleshy part of the nose for 10 minutes while they breathe through the mouth; if bleeding continues, reapply pressure for another 10 minutes; most nosebleeds stop within 20–30 minutes, and if it persists past half an hour seek medical attention; after it stops, advise rest for a few hours.
List the types of poisoning.::Medication (overdose of prescription or OTC drugs, especially opioids, sedatives and stimulants); chemical (cleaners, pesticides, paint thinners); food (contaminated food with bacteria, viruses or toxins); environmental (lead, mercury, asbestos); and carbon monoxide (toxic, can cause death at high concentrations) or carbon dioxide (not considered toxic but hazardous at large concentrations).
What first aid is given for swallowed poison?::Check that breathing is not impaired; if conscious, have the person rinse the mouth or take small sips of water only (no large amounts); do not induce vomiting and do not give any food, drink or medication.
What first aid is given for inhaled, contact and eye poison exposure?::Inhaled — move to fresh air, open windows and doors, loosen tight clothing and check breathing; contact — wear PPE, carefully remove contaminated clothing and wash the area with running water for at least 15–20 minutes; eye exposure — flush with cool, clean water without contaminating the other eye. If CPR is needed, use barrier devices or PPE.
When may you need to transport a casualty yourself?::When the environment is hazardous (fire, flooding, toxic fumes, collapse) or terrain unstable; in remote areas with limited access to emergency services; to reach specialised care faster; in temperature extremes or adverse weather; to prevent further injury or take pressure off injuries; to enable essential first aid or CPR; and to prepare evacuation in mass casualty situations.
When should you NOT move a casualty?::With spinal or neck injuries (unless there is an immediate threat), severe fractures or dislocations, or breathing difficulties or chest injuries — keep the casualty stable and wait for professional responders.
What are the general tips for transporting a casualty?::Assess the situation and scene safety first; check for spinal or neck injury before moving; professional transport is always preferred; maintain communication and reassure the casualty; move smoothly and slowly; and support the head and neck, especially after any trauma.

## 08 — Cardiac Arrest and You

What is the circulatory system?::Blood vessels that carry blood away from and towards the heart; it carries oxygen and nutrients to cells and removes waste products such as carbon dioxide.
What is the difference between arteries and veins?::Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart.
Describe the path of blood circulation.::Blood is pumped from the heart to the lungs to receive oxygen; the heart then sends the oxygenated blood through arteries to the rest of the body; veins carry the oxygen-poor blood back to the heart, and the circulation process starts over.
What would happen if the heart stops pumping blood?::Blood stops flowing through the body, so oxygen and nutrients no longer reach cells and waste products like carbon dioxide are not removed, causing organs to stop functioning properly (the subject of cardiac arrest care covered in later modules).

## 09 — Chain of Survival (Singapore)

What is the Chain of Survival?::A sequence or chain of events that must promptly occur to improve the chances of survival and recovery for victims of cardiac arrest.
List the links of the Chain of Survival in order.::Prevention; early activation and AED; early CPR; emergency medical services (EMS); early defibrillation; advanced cardiac life support (ACLS); recovery.
What does the first link, Prevention, emphasise?::Prevention is better than intervention — a healthy lifestyle reduces the risk of heart disease.
Why must CPR be initiated as soon as possible?::Brain damage begins in 4–6 minutes if blood flow to the brain is not resumed.
What role do EMS professionals play in the chain?::They provide critical medical interventions and transport the victim to hospital.
What does early defibrillation do?::Using an automated external defibrillator (AED) to shock the victim's heart improves survival rates significantly.
What is ACLS and when is it initiated?::Advanced cardiac life support, initiated promptly at the hospital.
What does the final link, Recovery, require?::Continued community and medical support to ensure a good survival outcome after cardiac arrest.

## 10 — Infant, Child and Adult CPR

Why is early CPR so important?::When the heart stops beating, blood stops flowing through the body; unless blood flow is restored, the other organs will stop functioning properly.
How do you check a casualty's responsiveness?::Tap the casualty's shoulders firmly and ask loudly, 'Hello! Hello! Are you okay?', avoiding violent shaking or movement of the neck.
What do you do after finding a casualty unresponsive?::Shout for help — get bystanders to call 995 for SCDF EMS or activate the emergency response system; if alone in a remote area with no access to EMS, check for breathing and pulse; ask bystanders to get an AED, and if alone continue with CPR until help arrives or the casualty recovers.
How do you check for normal breathing and pulse?::Look for the rise and fall of the chest wall and feel for a pulse simultaneously, within 10 seconds; if unsure, assume cardiac arrest and commence CPR.
How deep and fast should adult chest compressions be, per the CPR steps flowchart?::Compress the centre of the chest at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep at a rate of 100–120 compressions per minute, with the chest adequately exposed.
What is the compression-to-ventilation cycle and when do you reassess?::Complete 5 cycles of 30 compressions and 2 ventilations, then re-assess breathing and pulse after each set of 5 cycles.
What do you do if the AED advises a shock?::Stay clear and deliver the shock, then check for normal breathing and pulse; keep the casualty in the supine position and monitor; do not turn off the AED unless instructed by medical professionals or it stops working.
What acronym covers the AED hazard check before use?::WMG.
How long should the check for breathing and pulse take?::Not more than 10 seconds.
Where should you check the pulse in an infant versus a child or adult?::Infant — brachial pulse; child and adult — carotid pulse.
What is the compression landmark for CPR?::The centre of the chest at the lower half of the sternum (breastbone).
Give the compression depths by age group.::Infant — one-third of the anterior-posterior diameter of the chest (about 3–4 cm); child — 4–5 cm; adult — 4–6 cm.
What compression rate applies across all age groups?::100–120 compressions per minute.
What are the ventilation duration and volume guidance?::Each ventilation lasts 1 second; the chart lists 400–600 ml as the ventilation volume and otherwise instructs to ventilate until visible chest rise.
What ratios apply for a single versus multiple healthcare rescuers?::Single healthcare rescuer — 30:2, re-assess after 5 cycles; multiple healthcare rescuers — 15:2, re-assess after 10 cycles.
Give the rescue breathing rates by age group.::Infant — 1 breath per 2 seconds (30 breaths per minute); child — 1 breath per 3 seconds (20 breaths per minute); adult — 1 breath per 5 seconds (12 breaths per minute).

## 11 — CPR and AED

What does AED stand for?::Automated External Defibrillator
What is an AED?::A portable electronic device that detects life-threatening irregular heart rhythms and administers electric shocks (defibrillation) to help the heart start beating regularly
How many people suffer sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) in Singapore annually, and what proportion occurs in public spaces?::More than 3000 people, with 80% of cases occurring in public spaces
What is ventricular fibrillation (VF)?::A common rhythm associated with sudden cardiac arrest in which the heart muscles do not contract effectively and disrupt normal blood flow to the organs
What happens if normal heart rhythm and blood flow are not restored after SCA?::The chance of survival falls
What must everyone do during delivery of the AED electrical shock?::No one should touch the casualty; the operator repeats the AED's stand-clear command and ensures everyone is clear of the patient
What does the WMG mnemonic for AED dangers stand for?::Water, Metal, Gases (flammable gases)
Why should an AED not be used on a casualty with a wet chest?::Water is a good conductor and electricity takes the path of least resistance, so the shock would travel through the water instead of through the heart muscle
Why should an AED not be used where flammable gases have built up?::The sparks generated pose a risk of explosion and fire
Where must AED pads be placed relative to a pacemaker on the right side of the chest?::At least four fingers' breadth away from the device
What chest preparation is needed before applying AED pads?::Expose the chest; shave excessive chest hair if it interferes with pad placement; move metallic jewelry such as necklaces and chains away from the pads; remove medicated patches or monitoring electrodes that interfere; dry a wet or sweaty chest with a cloth
Where are AED pads placed on an adult?::One pad on the right side of the chest just below the collarbone, and the other pad just below and to the left of the left nipple
How are AED pads placed on an infant or child?::Anterior-posterior or anterior-anterior, making sure the pads are not touching each other and are at least 2 cm apart

## 12 — Foreign Body Airway Obstruction

What is an airway obstruction?::A blockage of any part of the airway that may partially or completely prevent air from getting to the lungs
What are the signs of a complete airway obstruction?::The casualty is unable to speak, breathe or cough effectively and may turn cyanotic; it is an emergency that may lead to death if not treated immediately
What does "cyanotic" mean?::Blue (bluish in colour)
What are the signs of a mild or partial airway obstruction?::The casualty can breathe but with some difficulty, may produce wheezing sounds as air passes through a narrower space, and is able to cough
What is the first aid for a conscious casualty with a partial airway obstruction?::Allow the casualty to clear their own airway by coughing, continuously check for deterioration (unable to produce sound, breathe or cough), and consider calling 995 for SCDF if repeated attempts are not successful
What is the first aid for a conscious casualty with a complete airway obstruction?::Perform abdominal thrusts, or chest thrusts for casualties who are obese, pregnant, or have abdominal injuries or operations, until the foreign object is dislodged or the casualty turns unconscious
What should you do if a casualty becomes unconscious during abdominal or chest thrusts?::Support and position the casualty supine on a firm, hard surface; activate EMS ("Help! Call 995 for SCDF and get an AED"); perform 30 chest compressions in the middle of the chest; open the airway and remove visible foreign objects; ventilate using the BVM to check for airway patency
What does BVM stand for?::Bag-valve-mask
After clearing the airway of an unconscious casualty with an obstruction, what do you check next?::If the airway is patent, check for breathing and pulse

## 13 — Post Incident Procedures

After successful resuscitation (return of spontaneous breathing and circulation), what should you do?::Do not move the casualty unless there is immediate danger; maintain the casualty in a supine position, check for any other injuries, and continue to monitor the casualty periodically
What should you do while waiting for SCDF paramedics to arrive?::Provide assurances to the casualty (if conscious) and family members according to RMG Service Standards, take the initiative to find out the casualty's medical history, and reassure them to reduce anxiety, fear or panic; bystanders may also need assurance that the situation is under control
According to what standards should assurances be given to the casualty and family?::RMG Service Standards
What questions may paramedics ask when they take over the casualty?::The time of collapse, whether an AED was used and how many shocks were given, and any previous medical history (if known)
Why should you reassure the casualty and family members after an incident?::To reduce their anxiety, fear or panic

## 14 — Road Traffic Accident (RTA)

What is a road traffic accident (RTA)?::A motor vehicle accident in which one or more vehicles collide with each other, with pedestrians or animals, or with stationary objects such as buildings, road dividers, or light posts
What does the ABCDE mnemonic stand for in RTA first aid?::Airway (with C-spine), Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure
What do you Look and Listen for when assessing Airway with C-spine in an RTA casualty?::Look without moving the casualty for obvious injuries to the neck and spine, noting that facial trauma, facial burns and haemorrhage may cause airway compromise; listen to how they sound, i.e. whether they can talk normally or have a hoarse voice or stridor
What is cyanosis?::Bluish discoloration of the extremities or lips
What is pneumothorax?::A condition where air enters the chest cavity, preventing proper expansion of the lung on the affected side and leading to uneven chest movements
What is flail chest?::A condition where multiple ribs located next to each other are fractured in two or more places, making the chest wall unstable and interfering with breathing
What is the rule regarding penetrating objects in external bleeding?::Do not remove any penetrating objects
What is haemothorax?::A condition where blood collects in the hollow area between the lungs and the rib cage (intra-thoracic bleeding)
What signs indicate intra-abdominal bleeding?::Abdominal distension, bruising, and tenderness
Why is a femur (long bone) fracture of particular concern in circulation assessment?::Because major blood vessels in the area can cause major bleeding
What should you look for even if there are no obvious signs of bleeding?::Signs of haemorrhagic or hypovolemic shock
How is Disability assessed in the ABCDE approach?::Using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) or the AVPU scale, and assessing whether the pupils are equal and reactive to light (PEARL)
What does PEARL stand for?::Pupils Equal And Reactive to Light
What are the four levels of the GCS Eye Opening scale?::4 opens spontaneously, 3 opens to sound, 2 opens to pain, 1 no response
What are the five levels of the GCS Verbal Response scale?::5 oriented (responds appropriately), 4 confused, 3 inappropriate words, 2 incomprehensible sounds, 1 no response
What are the six levels of the GCS Motor Response scale?::6 obeys commands, 5 localizes to pain, 4 withdraws from pain, 3 flexion to pain, 2 extension to pain, 1 no response
What does AVPU stand for?::Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive
In AVPU, what does each level mean?::Alert means fully awake and responding to questions appropriately; Verbal means responding to verbal stimuli such as speaking, shouting or verbal commands; Pain means responding to painful stimuli; Unresponsive means no response to any environmental, verbal or pain stimuli
What are the four general steps of first aid for an RTA?::Ensure safety and get help (protecting yourself and others, and considering safe extrication if there is immediate danger); assess the casualty using ABCDE and determine responsiveness; render appropriate first aid including stabilization and immobilization; arrange transport to a medical facility
What is rapid extrication and when is it used?::The removal of a person from a vehicle when the situation or condition demands it; it is used only in critical circumstances due to the potential risk of worsening injuries, especially spinal injuries
What are the primary indications for rapid extrication?::An unsafe environment (vehicle instability, or fire, smoke or hazardous materials); the casualty requires immediate assessment and care that cannot be done in the vehicle; the casualty requires immediate transport for critical trauma; or rescuers cannot access or stabilize vital functions due to vehicle constraints or the casualty blocking access to another seriously injured person
Give examples of critical trauma requiring immediate transport in rapid extrication.::Neurological deterioration, multi-trauma, and uncontrolled haemorrhage
What is a Halligan bar used for?::To pry or force a door or window open to gain entry
What is a spring-loaded window punch used for?::To break or shatter vehicle glass
What does KED stand for and what is it used for?::Kendrick Extrication Device, used to immobilize and extricate the casualty from confined spaces
What are spinal boards and skeds used for in extrication?::Spinal boards provide rigid support to transport a casualty; skeds or half-skeds are used to rapidly extricate from narrow spaces
What does the Exposure step of ABCDE involve in an RTA casualty?::Exposing and examining the casualty for other visible injuries such as deformity, bleeding or fractures, using a log roll technique if the casualty must be moved, and assessing body temperature, which may indicate blood loss or shock

## 15 — Spinal Management and Application of C-Collars

What does spinal management involve in first aid?::Keeping a casualty with a suspected spinal injury still and stabilizing the casualty's head and neck
What can cause spinal injuries?::Falling, diving, road traffic accidents, or sporting accidents
Why is special care given to a casualty with a suspected spinal injury?::To prevent further damage to the spinal cord and reduce the risk of permanent paralysis
List the signs and symptoms of a spinal injury.::Pressure in the head, neck or back; intense back pain; paralysis, lack of coordination or weakness in any part of the body; loss of sensation, numbness or tingling in the hands, feet, fingers or toes; twisted or oddly positioned neck or back; impaired breathing; loss of control of bladder or bowels; trouble walking or balancing
What is a cervical collar (C-collar)?::A neck brace used in first aid to immobilize the neck and support the head, minimizing further injury, particularly in suspected spinal injury
When should a C-collar be used?::When there are suspected spinal injuries, especially from trauma to the head, neck or back; after falls, car accidents or impact to the head; or when the casualty has numbness, tingling, weakness or loss of sensation in the limbs
What are the steps to apply a C-collar?::Ensure the area is safe and call emergency services if not already done; keep the casualty's head still using heavy towels or rolled sheets; maintain an open airway (if unconscious and not breathing begin CPR, if breathing but with impaired breathing gently align the head to a neutral position); if trained, apply a cervical collar to minimize neck movement
What are soft collars made of and how do they work?::Typically felt or foam materials cut to mould around the neck and jaw; because the collar sits under the chin it provides support and keeps the head in a normal position
What are rigid collars made of and what motion do they restrict?::Primarily plastic, making them easier to clean; they come in varying sizes and restrict motion in flexion and extension
Which type of C-collar provides more immobilization?::Rigid collars contributed to significantly more immobilization in all directions, although both rigid and soft collars limit neck motion in both directions
How do you select the correct C-collar size?::Hold your open hand on the patient's shoulders, record how many fingers it takes to reach the chin, and transfer that measurement to the front of the collar

## Abdominal Thrust (Heimlich Manoeuvre)

What are the signs and symptoms of choking (complete foreign body airway obstruction)?::Unable to speak, unable to breathe, unable to cough, and may be cyanotic (blue)
What should you ask a casualty you suspect is choking?::"Are you choking?"
If a casualty is unable to speak, breathe or cough, what should you say to them?::Tell the casualty that you can help
What are the first three steps of the abdominal thrust manoeuvre?::Stand behind the casualty, position yourself with your feet shoulder-width apart, and put your arms around the casualty's abdomen
How do you locate the correct hand position for abdominal thrusts?::Locate the navel with your ring finger and place 2 fingers above the navel
How is the fist formed and placed for an abdominal thrust?::Make a fist with the other hand with the thumb in the palm, then place the thumb side against the casualty's abdomen, above the 2 fingers
In what direction are abdominal thrusts given?::Quick inward and upward thrusts into the abdomen
How long should abdominal thrusts be performed?::Continuously until the foreign body is expelled or the casualty becomes unconscious
What is the alternative technique for a pregnant or obese casualty, and how does it differ?::Chest thrusts: stand behind the casualty, place your arms under the casualty's armpits, place the thumb side of a fist on the middle of the sternum, grasp the fist with the other hand, bring yourself close, and give quick backward thrusts until the foreign body is expelled or the casualty is unconscious
What should you do first when a choking casualty becomes unconscious?::Support and position the casualty lying on their back on a firm flat surface, then activate EMS by asking bystanders to call 995 for SCDF EMS and another to get an AED
What is the hand position for chest compressions on an unconscious choking casualty?::Place the heel of your palm on the lower half of the sternum, put your other hand on top with fingers interlocked, and start 30 chest compressions
How do you open the airway of an unconscious choking casualty?::Perform the head-tilt, chin-lift manoeuvre, then open the mouth to check for visible foreign bodies while maintaining the head tilt
How is a visible foreign body removed from an unconscious casualty's mouth?::Using the index finger to dislodge it (finger sweep)
How do you check whether the airway is clear after removing a foreign body?::Look for one successful chest rise by attempting one ventilation via a BVM (bag-valve-mask); if there is no resistance the airway is clear, otherwise reposition the head and re-attempt to ventilate
What do you do if there is still resistance after repositioning and re-attempting to ventilate?::Perform 30 chest compressions, check the mouth for any foreign body, and reassess airway patency
At what rate is rescue breathing given to a choking casualty with a pulse but no breathing?::12 breaths per minute, i.e. 1 breath every 5 seconds
What counting cadence is used between rescue breaths?::Give one breath and count "2-a-thousand", "3-a-thousand", "4-a-thousand", "5-a-thousand"
When should CPR be started on an unconscious choking casualty?::When both breathing and pulse are absent; if both are present, monitor the casualty until help arrives
