# Circulatory Injuries

## The Circulatory System
Also known as the cardiovascular system, it is a network responsible for transporting blood, nutrients, oxygen, carbon dioxide, hormones, and waste products throughout the body. It plays a crucial role in maintaining homeostasis, supplying cells with essential substances, and removing waste to keep the body functioning properly. The main components of the circulatory system include the **heart**, **blood vessels**, and **blood**.

### Functions
- **Transport of oxygen and nutrients** — delivers oxygen from the lungs and nutrients from the digestive system to cells throughout the body.
- **Removal of waste products** — carries waste products like carbon dioxide and metabolic byproducts to the lungs, liver, and kidneys for excretion.
- **Hormone distribution** — transports hormones from endocrine glands to target organs and tissues, regulating various body functions.
- **Temperature regulation** — distributes heat throughout the body and controls body temperature by adjusting blood flow to the skin.
- **Immune response** — transports white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection or injury.

### Components
- **Heart** — a muscular organ located in the chest that pumps blood throughout the body. It has four chambers:
  - **Right atrium** — receives deoxygenated blood from the body through the veins and pumps it to the right ventricle.
  - **Right ventricle** — pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs for oxygenation via the pulmonary arteries.
  - **Left atrium** — receives oxygenated blood from the lungs and pumps it to the left ventricle.
  - **Left ventricle** — pumps oxygenated blood to the rest of the body through the aorta, the largest artery.
- **Blood vessels** — the circulatory system contains three main types of blood vessels.
  - **Arteries** — carry oxygen-rich blood away from the heart to the body. The aorta is the main artery, and arteries branch out into smaller arterioles.
  - **Capillaries** — tiny blood vessels where the exchange of oxygen, nutrients, and waste occurs between blood and tissues.
  - **Veins** — carry deoxygenated blood back to the heart. Venules, the smallest veins, join to form larger veins. The superior and inferior vena cava are the main veins that bring blood back to the heart.
- **Blood** — the circulating fluid that transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products. It has four main components:
  - **Red blood cells (RBCs)** — contain haemoglobin, which binds oxygen and transports it to tissues.
  - **White blood cells (WBCs)** — part of the immune system, fighting infection and disease.
  - **Platelets** — help with blood clotting to prevent bleeding.
  - **Plasma** — the liquid component of blood, carrying nutrients, hormones, and waste products.

## Shock
Shock is a life-threatening medical condition in which the body's organs and tissues don't receive enough blood flow or oxygen to meet their needs. This inadequate blood supply can lead to cell and organ damage and, if not treated promptly, can result in organ failure and even death. Shock can have various causes and presents in different types, each requiring specific medical interventions.

### Types of Shock
**Hypovolemic shock** — occurs when there is a significant loss of blood or body fluids, such as from severe bleeding (haemorrhage), dehydration (from vomiting, diarrhoea, or excessive sweating), or fluid loss due to burns.
- Signs & symptoms:
  - Profuse bleeding
  - Sweating
  - Dizziness
  - Confusion
  - Shallow breathing
  - Black stools*
  - Abdominal pain*
  - Vomiting blood*
  - *if bleeding is internal
- Control measures:
  - Control haemorrhages and fractures
  - Relieve pain and maintain body heat
  - Monitor vital signs
  - Lay flat on the floor and elevate legs
  - Seek emergency care

**Cardiogenic shock** — occurs when the heart is unable to pump blood effectively due to damage or disease, such as in heart attacks (myocardial infarction), heart failure, or severe arrhythmias.
- Signs & symptoms:
  - Sudden increase in heart rate
  - Shortness of breath
  - Sweating
  - Pale skin
  - Faintness
- Control measures:
  - Seek emergency care
  - Monitor vital signs
  - Perform CPR if the victim stops breathing
  - Maintain body heat and loosen tight clothing

**Septic shock** — resulting from a severe infection that spreads throughout the body, often causing a drop in blood pressure.
- Signs & symptoms:
  - Confusion
  - Cyanosis
  - Dizziness
  - Breathing problems
  - Fever
  - Increased heart rate
- Control measures:
  - Seek emergency care
  - Monitor vital signs
  - Lay flat on the floor and elevate legs
  - If the victim is struggling to breathe, allow him to sit up, not stand
  - Perform CPR if the victim stops breathing

**Anaphylactic shock** — a severe allergic reaction that causes blood vessels to dilate and airways to constrict.
- Signs & symptoms:
  - Constricted airways, may impair breathing
  - Swollen tongue and lips
  - Flushed skin
  - Confusion
  - Dizziness
  - Tingling sensation
- Control measures:
  - Seek emergency care
  - Monitor vital signs
  - Lay flat on the floor and elevate legs
  - If the victim is struggling to breathe, allow him to sit up, not stand
  - Perform CPR if the victim stops breathing

**Neurogenic shock** — caused by damage to the nervous system, such as spinal cord injuries, leading to a loss of vascular tone and low blood pressure.
- Signs & symptoms:
  - Fainting
  - Sudden low blood pressure
  - Warm/flushed skin due to vasodilation
  - Slower heart rate
- Control measures:
  - Seek emergency care
  - Monitor the victim's vital signs
  - Do not move the victim with suspected spinal injury
  - Perform CPR if the victim stops breathing

> [!Warning]- Treatment for Shock _(Trained healthcare personnel only)_
> Treatment depends on the underlying cause of shock but generally includes:
> 1. **Fluid resuscitation** — intravenous fluids (IV fluids) are often given to restore blood volume and improve circulation. In some cases, blood transfusions may be necessary to treat blood loss.
> 2. **Oxygen therapy** — to ensure that the body's tissues receive adequate oxygen, supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation may be required.
> 3. **Medications** — medications may be administered to stabilize blood pressure, such as vasopressors (for vasodilation) or inotropes (to increase heart contractility).
> 4. **Treating the underlying cause** — if the shock is due to an infection (sepsis), antibiotics may be given. If it's due to an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), epinephrine (adrenaline) may be used. In cases of trauma, surgery may be required to stop bleeding or treat injuries.

## Bleeding
Bleeding, or haemorrhage, is the loss of blood from the circulatory system due to injury or damage to blood vessels. Bleeding can occur internally, within the body, or externally, through natural openings (such as the mouth, nose, or rectum) or breaks in the skin. Prompt recognition and control of bleeding are essential, as excessive blood loss can lead to shock, organ failure, and even death if untreated. Bleeding can be classified as external or internal.

**External bleeding** — blood escapes the body through a wound on the skin surface.
- **Capillary bleeding** — the mildest type, usually from small cuts or scrapes, where blood oozes slowly from capillaries (tiny blood vessels).
- **Venous bleeding** — bleeding from a vein; it flows steadily and may appear dark red due to lower oxygen levels.
- **Arterial bleeding** — the most severe form, where blood spurts out in rhythm with the heartbeat. It is bright red and can be life-threatening due to rapid blood loss.

**Internal bleeding** — blood leaks within the body due to trauma, disease, or ruptured blood vessels. It can be difficult to detect and is often serious.
- **Causes** — trauma, fractures, ruptured organs, aneurysms, or gastrointestinal conditions.
- **Symptoms** — pain, swelling, bruising, dizziness, confusion, and signs of shock (e.g. pale skin, rapid pulse, low blood pressure).

### Signs of Significant Blood Loss
- **Rapid pulse** — the heart beats faster to compensate for lost blood volume.
- **Low blood pressure** — a drop in blood volume can cause blood pressure to fall.
- **Pale, cool, clammy skin** — blood is redirected from the skin to vital organs.
- **Weakness or dizziness** — due to reduced blood flow to the brain.
- **Confusion or loss of consciousness** — in severe cases, due to lack of oxygen in the brain.

### First Aid for External Bleeding
1. Apply direct pressure to the wound using gloved hands and place a clean dressing over the wound as soon as possible. Direct pressure can help control minor to moderate bleeding.
2. Apply a bulky pad extending beyond the edges of the wound, and bandage firmly. If bleeding continues, leave the dressing in place and bandage another one on top.
3. Do not disturb the pads or bandages once the bleeding is controlled.

### First Aid for Internal Bleeding
1. Call for SCDF at 995.
2. Perform primary and secondary survey.
3. Lay the casualty down and raise their legs above the level of the heart, unless there is a fracture in the lower limb, pelvis, or a spinal injury.
4. Loosen tight clothing and give nothing by mouth — do not allow them to eat or drink.
5. Commence CPR and use an AED if the casualty is not breathing.
6. Reassure the casualty and do not leave them unattended.
7. Monitor the casualty and record vital signs at regular intervals.

## Wounds
### Types of Wounds
- **Abrasions** — abrasions or grazes tend to be caused by a rough object or surface. When the bleeding has stopped, flush the area with sterile water to remove any foreign particles.
- **Lacerations** — wounds with jagged edges. There is more tissue damage, resulting in scarring. Lacerations may also result in moderate to heavy bleeding.
- **Incisions** — clean-edged cuts such as those caused by a sharp knife or glass. Incisions may look harmless but there can be considerable damage to underlying tendons, nerves, blood vessels etc.
- **Amputations** — partial or complete severance of a body part. Focus on controlling the bleeding prior to preserving the amputated limb using an ice pack. Do not let the amputated part come into direct contact with ice.
- **Punctures** — typically caused by long objects, making them tricky to assess. Do not remove any embedded object. If bleeding occurs and is severe, control bleeding by applying pressure around the object.
- **Nosebleeds** — nosebleeds or epistaxis occur when tiny blood vessels in the nose burst. They may be caused by trauma, irritation or dryness of the lining of the nose, colds, or sinusitis. Apply pressure at the soft part of the nose for about 10 minutes.
- **Bee stings** — bee stings cause pain and swelling to the affected site. If the stinger is still embedded, remove it by scraping. Wash the wound with soap and water and apply an ice pack. Monitor for allergic reactions, e.g. rashes, swelling, breathlessness. Seek immediate medical attention if allergic reactions occur, or if you have been stung multiple times.
- **Snake bites** — snake bites should be treated as life-threatening, as a venomous snake bite may cause abnormal bleeding or affect the normal functions of the body systems. Call for SCDF 995 and remove accessories which may cause obstruction when there's swelling. Keep the affected area below the level of the heart. Ensure the casualty remains calm. Take a photo of the snake when safely possible.
- **Jellyfish stings** — jellyfish have stinging cells inside their tentacles for protection. When stung, sit the casualty down and call for SCDF 995. Pour vinegar or sea water over the site for at least 30 seconds. Vinegar is a weak acid that might keep the stingers from firing. For pain relief, immerse the sting site in hot water for 10–20 minutes. Monitor the casualty for reactions to the venom such as difficulty in breathing.

## Dressing and Bandaging
Dressings and bandages are essential components of wound care. They help protect the wound, absorb exudate, reduce the risk of infection, and promote healing. While the terms are sometimes used interchangeably, they serve distinct purposes.
- **Dressings** — sterile materials applied directly to the wound to protect it, absorb fluids, and aid in the healing process.
- **Bandages** — used to secure the dressing in place, provide additional support, and help maintain pressure if needed.

### Types of Dressings
- **Gauze dressings** — made from woven cotton, these are common, versatile dressings used for most wounds. They can be used as primary dressings on top of wounds or as secondary layers for extra absorption.
- **Non-adherent dressings** — these do not stick to the wound bed, making them suitable for sensitive or healing wounds where minimal trauma is essential during dressing changes.
- **Hydrocolloid dressings** — maintain a moist wound environment, ideal for healing. They are used for wounds with mild to moderate exudate.
- **Foam dressings** — made from foam, these dressings provide cushioning and are highly absorbent, making them ideal for wounds with significant drainage.
- **Transparent film dressings** — thin, adhesive dressings that are waterproof and provide a barrier while allowing visibility of the wound. They are often used for superficial wounds or as secondary dressings.
- **Hydrogel dressings** — keep wounds hydrated, making them ideal for dry or necrotic (dead tissue) wounds. They provide a cooling effect and are used for burns or painful wounds.

### Types of Bandages
- **Roller bandages** — long strips of material (usually cotton or elastic) rolled into a coil, used to secure dressings in place, apply pressure, or immobilize joints. Types include:
  - **Crepe bandages** — elastic, often used for sprains or to secure dressings on limbs.
  - **Gauze roller bandages** — non-elastic, often used for light support or to hold dressings in place.
- **Triangular bandages** — large, triangular pieces of cloth used as slings for support or to secure larger areas like shoulders or limbs. They are also versatile for creating makeshift bandages or splints in emergencies.
- **Tubular bandages** — elastic, tube-shaped bandages designed to fit around limbs or joints, providing light compression and support. They're often used for fingers, toes, or joints.
- **Elastic (compression) bandages** — stretchy bandages, like Ace bandages, that provide support and reduce swelling. They are commonly used for sprains, strains, and to control bleeding by maintaining pressure.
- **Adhesive bandages** — self-adhesive strips, like Band-Aids, used for minor cuts or abrasions. They contain a small pad that covers the wound and an adhesive strip to secure it.

### Purpose and Function
- **Protection** — dressings shield wounds from dirt, bacteria, and contaminants, reducing the risk of infection.
- **Absorption** — dressings absorb wound exudate, preventing maceration (skin softening from moisture) and maintaining a clean environment.
- **Pressure** — bandages can apply pressure to control bleeding or reduce swelling in injured areas.
- **Immobilization** — bandages help immobilize the injured area, especially for sprains, strains, or fractures, to prevent further damage.
- **Support and comfort** — bandages and dressings help stabilize the wound, reduce pain, and allow for easier movement and comfort.

### Steps for Applying Dressings/Bandages
1. **Clean the wound** — before applying a dressing, clean the wound with water or saline to remove debris.
2. **Apply the dressing** — place a sterile dressing over the wound, ensuring it covers the entire area.
3. **Secure with a bandage** — use an appropriate bandage to hold the dressing in place. Wrap firmly but not too tightly to avoid restricting blood flow.
4. **Check for circulation** — after applying a bandage, ensure that blood flow is not compromised by checking for skin color, warmth, and sensation in the area.

> [!NOTE]- Note
> - Use sterile dressings — always use sterile dressings to minimize the risk of infection.
> - Change dressings regularly — replace dressings as per medical guidance or when they become soiled or saturated.
> - Watch for infection signs — redness, swelling, heat, or discharge may indicate infection, requiring prompt attention.

### Folding a Broad Bandage
1. Lay the triangular bandage flat.
2. First fold: fold the edge of the bandage (apex) toward the center.
3. Second fold: fold the opposite edge toward the center.

### Folding a Narrow Bandage
1. Lay the triangular bandage flat.
2. First fold: fold the edge of the bandage (apex) toward the center.
3. Second fold: fold the opposite edge toward the center.
4. Third fold: fold in half again.

## Tourniquet
A tourniquet is a tight band applied around a limb to control severe bleeding by stopping blood flow to the area. Tourniquets are powerful tools and can be lifesaving when used correctly, but they should only be applied in specific situations, as misuse can lead to serious complications.

### When to Apply a Tourniquet
- **Severe, life-threatening bleeding** — when there is uncontrollable bleeding from a limb (arm or leg) that cannot be stopped by direct pressure, a tourniquet may be necessary. This type of bleeding often includes:
  - Blood spurting out rhythmically (arterial bleeding)
  - Large open wounds with extensive tissue damage
  - Complete or partial limb amputation
- **Inability to control bleeding by other means** — if bleeding continues despite applying direct pressure, elevating the limb, or using a pressure dressing, a tourniquet may be the only option left to prevent blood loss.
- **When direct pressure is not possible** — in situations where direct pressure cannot be maintained, such as:
  - The person is in a dangerous or unstable environment, and immediate evacuation is needed
  - The injury is too extensive for direct pressure to be effective
- **Mass-casualty situations** — in emergency situations with multiple casualties (e.g. natural disasters or accidents) where medical resources are limited, a tourniquet may be applied to prioritize stopping life-threatening bleeding quickly.

> [!warning]- Note
> - **Do not** use a tourniquet for minor bleeding or if bleeding can be controlled with direct pressure, elevation, or a pressure dressing.
> - Tourniquets are only for limbs (arms and legs) as they cut off blood supply entirely. They cannot be used effectively on other parts of the body.
> - Prolonged use of a tourniquet can cause nerve damage, tissue damage, and potentially the loss of the limb. Therefore, tourniquets should only be used when absolutely necessary, as the lack of blood flow can result in complications.

### Applying a Tourniquet
1. Tie the broad bandage with a half hitch about 5 cm above the injury.
2. Place a stick in place with another half hitch.
3. Twist the stick to tighten; stop and secure once bleeding stops.
4. Record the time of application.
