# Other First Aid Knowledge

## Eye
### Structure and Functions
![eye-anatomy.jpg](/img/user/Study/First%20Aid%20Course/Images/eye-anatomy.jpg)

The structure of the eye is complex and designed to focus light, enabling vision. The eye consists of several key parts, each with a specific role in capturing light, focusing it, and transmitting visual information to the brain.
- **Light capture** — the cornea and lens focus light onto the retina.
- **Image detection** — photoreceptors in the retina detect light and convert it into electrical signals.
- **Signal transmission** — the optic nerve transmits signals to the brain.
- **Image interpretation** — the visual cortex processes these signals, creating the images we see.

### Penetrating Eye Injuries
Penetrating eye injuries occur when a foreign object pierces the outer layer of the eye, entering into or through deeper structures. These injuries are serious and can lead to vision loss, infection, or other complications if not treated promptly. Penetrating eye injuries often involve the cornea or sclera and can reach the inner eye structures.

**Signs & symptoms:**
- Severe eye pain
- Decreased or blurred vision
- Visible foreign body or wound on the eye
- Blood within the eye (hyphema)
- Tears in the iris, lens, or cornea
- Leakage of clear or bloody fluid from the eye
- Eye swelling and bruising around the eye

**First aid:**
Treatment generally includes surgical repair, antibiotics to prevent infection, and close follow-up to monitor healing and address complications like infection, retinal detachment, or vision loss.
1. Protect the injured eye with an eye pad or eye shield.
2. Cover the uninjured eye with a patch to minimize eye movement.
3. Call EMS at 995.
4. If the object is too large to be covered by an eye pad or eye shield, apply techniques for dealing with penetrating objects.
5. Do **not** remove any object stuck in the eye.
6. Do **not** apply too much pressure to the uninjured eye.

### Chemical Injuries to the Eye
Chemical injuries to the eye occur when harmful substances come into contact with the eye's surface, leading to irritation, inflammation, or even severe damage to the eye structures. These injuries can result from exposure to acids, alkalis, or other toxic chemicals commonly found in household cleaning products, industrial chemicals, or lab substances. Chemical injuries are considered eye emergencies, as rapid treatment is essential to minimize long-term damage.

**Signs & symptoms:**
- Intense pain and burning in the eye
- Redness and inflammation
- Tearing or watery discharge
- Blurred vision or visual impairment
- Eyelid spasms (blepharospasm)
- Possible whitening or clouding of the cornea in severe cases

**Types of chemicals and their effects:**
**Acidic chemicals:**
- Examples: sulfuric acid (found in car batteries), hydrochloric acid, vinegar
- Effects: cause immediate pain and burning. Acidic substances tend to cause coagulation of proteins on the eye's surface, which can sometimes limit the depth of damage but may still harm the cornea.

**Alkaline chemicals:**
- Examples: ammonia, sodium hydroxide (lye), lime (from cement)
- Effects: alkalis penetrate the eye more quickly and deeply than acids, often causing more severe damage. They can saponify fats in cell membranes, allowing deeper penetration and causing more extensive damage.

**First aid:**
- **Irrigate the eye** — rinse the eye immediately with copious amounts of clean water or saline solution.
  - Irrigate from the nose side of the eye outwards to avoid flushing the chemical into the other eye.
  - Continue flushing the eye for at least 15–20 minutes to dilute and wash away the chemical.
  - Encourage the casualty to roll their eyeball so that the water can flush out the chemical from all parts of the eye.
  - For severe cases, irrigation may need to continue longer, especially if the chemical is an alkali.
- **Remove contact lenses** — if the person is wearing contact lenses, remove them as soon as possible, as they can trap chemicals against the eye.
- **Avoid rubbing the eye** — rubbing can cause further damage.
- **Seek immediate medical attention** — after initial rinsing, prompt evaluation by an eye care professional is essential for proper assessment and further treatment.

### Foreign Objects in the Eye
A foreign object in the eye is when any external particle or material becomes lodged in or on the eye's surface. This could include dirt, dust, metal shavings, wood chips, eyelashes, or other small particles. Depending on the object's location and size, it can cause varying degrees of discomfort and potential damage to the eye.

**Types:**
- **Superficial objects** — on the surface of the eye, such as the conjunctiva (the thin membrane covering the eye) or the cornea.
- **Embedded objects** — penetrate deeper into the cornea or other parts of the eye, requiring immediate medical attention.
- **Organic material** — particles like plant material, dust, or dirt that can increase the risk of infection if not removed promptly.

**Signs & symptoms:**
- Sensation of something in the eye
- Pain or discomfort in the affected eye
- Redness and tearing
- Blurred vision or difficulty seeing clearly
- Increased sensitivity to light
- Scratching or itching sensation
- Possible swelling of the eyelid

**First aid for embedded foreign objects:**
- Protect the injured eye with an eye pad or eye shield to prevent the object from being driven deeper into the eye.
- Cover the uninjured eye with a patch to stop eye movement.
- Call SCDF at 995.
- Do **not** allow the casualty to rub the eye.
- Do **not** attempt to remove any embedded object.
- Do **not** use dry cotton (cotton balls or cotton-tipped swabs) or instruments such as tweezers on the eye.

**Complications (if left untreated):**
- **Corneal abrasions** — scratches on the eye surface, which can be painful and increase infection risk.
- **Infections** — particularly with organic materials, infections like conjunctivitis or keratitis may develop.
- **Scarring** — in severe cases, scarring can impair vision.
- **Vision impairment** — if significant damage occurs to the cornea or other parts of the eye, vision may be permanently affected.

### Cuts to the Eye and Eyelids
Cuts (lacerations) to the eye or eyelids can occur from trauma or accidents involving sharp objects, fingernails, glass, or any other materials that can create a tear or puncture. Eye and eyelid lacerations are serious injuries and often require immediate medical attention to prevent complications and preserve vision.

**Types of cuts:**
1. **Superficial eyelid lacerations** — small cuts on the surface of the eyelid that may not affect the deeper layers.
2. **Full-thickness eyelid lacerations** — cuts that extend through the entire eyelid and may involve the eye muscles or other structures.
3. **Corneal lacerations** — a cut on the cornea (the clear, outer layer of the eye), often caused by sharp objects. Corneal lacerations are particularly serious as they can damage vision.
4. **Scleral lacerations** — a cut in the sclera (the white part of the eye), often indicating a deeper injury and potentially involving the retina or other internal structures.

**Signs & symptoms:**
- Pain and discomfort in the affected area
- Visible cut or wound on the eyelid or eye surface
- Swelling and bruising around the injury site
- Redness or bleeding from the eye or eyelid
- Blurred or decreased vision if the eye is involved
- Sensitivity to light
- Eye watering or discharge

**First aid:**
- **Avoid rubbing or pressing the eye** — rubbing can worsen the injury.
- **Cover the eye** — lightly cover the eye with a clean, sterile bandage or eye shield to protect it from further injury. Avoid pressing directly on the wound.
- **Seek immediate medical attention** — eye lacerations are an emergency; prompt evaluation by an eye care professional or in the emergency room is essential.
- **Avoid rinsing the eye** — if there is a deep cut on the eye itself, avoid rinsing as it could worsen the injury.

## Nosebleeds (Epistaxis)
A nosebleed occurs when there is bleeding from the tissues inside the nose, often from ruptured blood vessels in the nasal mucosa. Nosebleeds are common and can range from mild to severe, with causes varying widely.

**Types:**
- **Anterior nosebleeds** — the most common type; occur from the front part of the nose, usually from the blood vessels in the nasal septum. They are often mild and easy to control.
- **Posterior nosebleeds** — less common but more serious, as they originate from deeper blood vessels in the nose. Posterior nosebleeds are more challenging to control and may require medical intervention.

**Causes:**
- **Dry air** — can dry out the nasal membranes, making them more susceptible to cracking and bleeding.
- **Nasal trauma** — from nose picking, blowing the nose too hard, or an injury to the nose.
- **Allergic rhinitis or respiratory infections** — inflammation and irritation in the nasal passages can lead to bleeding.
- **Medications** — blood thinners, anticoagulants, or frequent use of nasal sprays can increase the likelihood of nosebleeds.
- **Hypertension (high blood pressure)** — though not a direct cause, high blood pressure can make nosebleeds more difficult to stop.
- **Structural abnormalities** — conditions like a deviated septum or nasal polyps can predispose a person to epistaxis.
- **Underlying health conditions** — rarely, bleeding disorders or certain cancers can lead to nosebleeds.

**Signs & symptoms:**
- Bleeding from one or both nostrils
- Sensation of fluid (blood) running down the back of the throat if bleeding is heavy
- Potential lightheadedness or dizziness with severe blood loss (rare in mild cases)

**First aid:**
- Sit the casualty down with their head slightly forward.
- Help the casualty pinch the fleshy part of the nose for 10 minutes, asking them to breathe through their open mouth.
- If the bleeding continues, reapply the pressure for another 10 minutes.
- Most nosebleeds should stop within 20 to 30 minutes. If it persists for more than half an hour, seek medical attention.
- After the bleeding has stopped, advise the casualty to rest for a few hours.

## Poisoning
Poisoning occurs when a person is exposed to a substance that can lead to health complications or even death. Poisoning can be accidental or intentional and includes exposure to household chemicals, medications, industrial chemicals, or environmental toxins.

**Types:**
- **Medication** — overdose from prescription or over-the-counter (OTC) drugs, especially opioids, sedatives, and stimulants.
- **Chemical** — exposure to harmful household or industrial chemicals, such as cleaners, pesticides, or paint thinners.
- **Food** — ingestion of contaminated food containing bacteria, viruses, or toxins.
- **Environmental** — exposure to toxins in the environment such as lead, mercury, or asbestos.
- **Carbon monoxide (CO) or carbon dioxide (CO₂)** — CO is toxic to the body and can cause death if breathed in at high concentrations. CO₂ isn't considered toxic but can pose health hazards at large concentrations.

**Signs & symptoms:**
Symptoms vary according to the substance and concentration but usually include:
- Nausea or vomiting
- Dizziness or confusion
- Seizures
- Breathing difficulty
- Loss of consciousness

**First aid:**
First aid is crucial in preventing further harm and stabilizing the casualty until advanced medical care arrives.
- Ensure that the area is safe.
- Try to identify the type of poison (e.g. medication, chemical, food). Check if there are containers or clues that may provide information on the substance.
- Call for emergency services immediately, especially if symptoms are severe or the casualty is unconscious. Provide details to the operator, including the substance involved (e.g. chemical) and the amount ingested or inhaled (if known).
- **Swallowed poison** — check if breathing is impaired. Advise the person to rinse the mouth (if conscious) or offer small sips of water (avoid giving large amounts). Do not induce vomiting or give any food, drink, or medication.
- **Inhaled poison** — move the person to an area with fresh air. Open windows and doors if indoors. Loosen tight clothing and check the quality and rate of breathing.
- **Contact poisoning** — wear appropriate PPE. Carefully remove contaminated clothing and avoid contact with the substance. Wash the affected area with running water for at least 15–20 minutes.
- **Eye exposure** — flush the affected eye with cool, clean water. Avoid contaminating the unaffected eye.
- Monitor the casualty's condition while waiting for help to arrive. Observe for changes in breathing, consciousness, and responsiveness.
- Perform CPR if the casualty is unconscious and not breathing normally. Use appropriate protective equipment or barrier devices so you do not come into contact with the substance.

**Preventive measures:**
- Store chemicals, medicines, and potentially harmful substances out of children's reach.
- Use labels and keep chemicals in original containers.

## Transporting a Casualty
Transporting a casualty requires careful attention to prevent further injury and ensure safety. Proper techniques vary depending on the nature and severity of the injury, and whether professional help is available.

**General guidelines:**
- **Assess the situation first** — ensure the scene is safe for you to approach and evaluate the casualty's condition.
- **Check for spinal or neck injuries** — if there's any indication of a spinal injury (from falls, car accidents, or head trauma), avoid moving the casualty unless absolutely necessary.
- **Call for emergency services** — if available, professional transportation by trained personnel is always preferred.

### Why and When to Transport
Transporting a casualty may be necessary for several reasons, particularly in emergency situations where the casualty's condition or the surrounding environment poses a threat to their safety or survival:
1. **Move them to a safer location**
   - **Hazardous environment** — in situations involving fire, flooding, toxic fumes, or potential building collapse, it may be essential to move the casualty to prevent further harm.
   - **Unstable terrain** — if the casualty is injured in a location where the ground is unstable, such as on a cliff, hill, or slippery surface, moving them can prevent additional accidents or injuries.
2. **Provide better access to medical help**
   - **Limited access to emergency services** — in remote areas, it may be necessary to transport the casualty to a place where emergency medical services can reach them more easily.
   - **Get to specialized care** — sometimes, it's essential to bring the casualty closer to medical facilities, even if emergency help is on the way, to reduce the delay in receiving specialized treatment.
3. **Protect against deteriorating conditions**
   - **Temperature extremes** — if the casualty is exposed to extreme heat or cold, you may need to move them to prevent conditions like hypothermia or heatstroke from developing or worsening.
   - **Adverse weather** — rain, snow, and strong winds can worsen the casualty's condition, particularly if they have open wounds, are in shock, or are unable to regulate their body temperature effectively.
4. **Prevent further injury or complications**
   - **Risk of additional injury** — in some cases, leaving the casualty in the current location could worsen their injury. Moving them to a more stable, secure place can help reduce risks.
   - **Avoid pressure on injuries** — for casualties with injuries that might be aggravated by their current position, gentle repositioning or transportation may be necessary.
5. **Facilitate essential first aid or CPR**
   - **Inaccessible position** — if the casualty is in a position that makes it challenging to perform necessary first aid, CPR, or other life-saving techniques, repositioning them can make it easier for you or others to administer help.
6. **Prepare for evacuation in large-scale emergencies**
   - **Mass casualty situations** — in situations like natural disasters, car accidents, or other large-scale emergencies, quickly moving casualties away from the area can help streamline emergency response and triage efforts.

### Additional Tips
- **Maintain communication** — reassure the casualty, let them know what you're doing, and ask for feedback on any pain.
- **Move smoothly and slowly** — sudden movements can aggravate injuries.
- **Positioning** — ensure that the head and neck are supported, especially if there is any trauma.

### When Not to Move the Casualty
- **Spinal or neck injuries** — unless there is an immediate threat, wait for professional responders.
- **Severe fractures or dislocations** — moving can worsen injuries; stabilize the limb and wait for help if possible.
- **Breathing difficulties or chest injuries** — movement could worsen these conditions, so it's best to keep the casualty stable.
