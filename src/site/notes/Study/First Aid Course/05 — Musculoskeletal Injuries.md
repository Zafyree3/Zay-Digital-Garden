# Musculoskeletal Injuries

## The Musculoskeletal System
The musculoskeletal system is the body system responsible for movement, support, stability, and protection of vital organs. It consists of bones, muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments, and other connective tissues that work together to give the body its form, protect internal structures, and enable physical movement.

### Parts of the System
- **Muscles** — tissues that contract to produce movement. They are attached to bones and generate the force needed for motion. There are three main types:
  - **Skeletal muscles** — attached to bones, these are voluntary muscles controlled consciously to move body parts.
  - **Smooth muscles** — found in organs and blood vessels, these are involuntary muscles that regulate functions like digestion and blood flow.
  - **Cardiac muscle** — the specialized involuntary muscle that makes up the heart, allowing it to pump blood.
- **Tendons** — strong, fibrous tissues that connect muscles to bones. They transmit the force generated by muscles to move bones, allowing joint movement.
- **Bones** — rigid structures that form the skeleton and provide the framework of the body. They protect vital organs (e.g. the skull protects the brain, and the ribcage protects the heart and lungs).
  - Bones also store minerals (like calcium and phosphorus) and produce blood cells in the bone marrow.
- **Ligaments** — bands of connective tissue that connect bones to each other at joints, providing stability and limiting excessive movement to prevent injury.
- **Cartilage** — a flexible, rubbery tissue that covers and cushions the ends of bones at joints. It reduces friction, absorbs shock, and supports structures like the nose, ears, and airways.

### Functions
- **Movement** — muscles contract to pull on bones, creating movement. The joints allow different ranges and types of motion.
- **Support and stability** — bones form the body's framework, giving shape and providing support. Ligaments, tendons, and muscles contribute to joint stability.
- **Protection of organs** — bones protect internal organs from injury (e.g. the skull protects the brain, and the rib cage shields the heart and lungs).
- **Mineral storage** — bones store essential minerals, especially calcium and phosphorus, which are released into the bloodstream as needed for other bodily functions.
- **Blood cell production** — bone marrow, found in certain bones, produces blood cells, including red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.
- **Heat production** — muscles generate heat during contraction, which helps regulate body temperature.

## Fractures
Fractures are breaks or cracks in bones that occur when the bone is subjected to more force than it can handle. Fractures vary widely in severity, from small cracks to complete breaks. They are often caused by trauma, falls, accidents, or repetitive stress, but certain medical conditions, like osteoporosis, can make bones more susceptible to fractures.

### Types of Fractures
- **Simple (closed) fracture** — the bone is broken, but there is no open wound in the skin. The broken bone does not puncture the skin, reducing the risk of infection.
- **Compound (open) fracture** — the broken bone pierces through the skin, creating an open wound. This type of fracture carries a high risk of infection and requires prompt medical treatment.
- **Complete fracture** — the bone is broken all the way through, separating it into two or more parts.
- **Incomplete (partial) fracture** — the bone is not broken all the way through. Types include:
  - **Greenstick fracture** — common in children, where one side of the bone bends and cracks while the other side remains intact.
  - **Hairline (stress) fracture** — a thin crack in the bone, often caused by repetitive stress or overuse.
- **Comminuted fracture** — the bone is broken into multiple pieces or fragments. This type of fracture is often due to high-impact trauma.
- **Transverse fracture** — the break is in a straight line across the bone, typically resulting from a direct blow or impact.
- **Oblique fracture** — the break is at an angle across the bone. Oblique fractures are often caused by a sharp, angled blow to the bone.
- **Spiral fracture** — the fracture spirals around the bone, often caused by a twisting or rotational force. This type of fracture is commonly seen in sports injuries.
- **Compression fracture** — the bone is crushed, often seen in the vertebrae (spine). Compression fractures are common in people with osteoporosis.
- **Avulsion fracture** — a small piece of bone is pulled away from the main bone by a tendon or ligament. This type of fracture is usually seen in sports injuries.
- **Pathologic fracture** — a fracture that occurs in a bone weakened by disease, such as osteoporosis, cancer, or infection.

### Symptoms of Fractures
- **Pain** — severe, localized pain at the site of the fracture.
- **Swelling and bruising** — the area around the fracture may swell and bruise due to blood vessel damage.
- **Deformity** — the limb may look twisted, bent, or out of place if the fracture has caused misalignment.
- **Loss of function** — difficulty moving the affected limb or bearing weight.
- **Crepitus** — a grating or grinding sound when moving the broken limb, due to bone fragments rubbing together.

### Complications of Fractures
- **Infection** — particularly in open fractures, where the wound is exposed to bacteria.
- **Nonunion or malunion** — failure of the bone to heal properly, resulting in a nonunion (no healing) or malunion (healing in the wrong position).
- **Nerve or blood vessel damage** — fractures can sometimes damage nearby nerves or blood vessels, leading to complications.
- **Compartment syndrome** — swelling in the muscles around the fracture can increase pressure and reduce blood flow; a medical emergency that requires prompt treatment.

### First Aid for Fractures
1. For open fractures, cover the wound with a dressing and apply pressure around the area to control bleeding. Do not apply pressure on the bone.
2. Call SCDF at 995. If there's no immediate danger, do not move/splint the fractured limb and wait for paramedics.
3. Reassure the casualty and inform them not to move.
4. Immobilize/splint the injured area and treat for associated injuries/shock.
5. Do not move the casualty until the injured part is secured and supported.
6. Check for signs of impaired circulation.
7. Do not allow the casualty to eat or drink anything.

## Dislocations
A dislocation is an injury in which the bones in a joint are forced out of their normal positions. This often occurs due to trauma or impact, causing one of the bones in a joint to slip out of alignment. Dislocations can be extremely painful, restricting movement and sometimes damaging surrounding ligaments, tendons, muscles, and nerves.

### Types of Dislocations
- **Shoulder dislocation** — the shoulder is the most commonly dislocated joint due to its wide range of motion and relatively shallow socket. It can be dislocated forward, backward, or downward, with anterior dislocations (forward) being the most common.
- **Elbow dislocation** — often caused by a fall or accident, elbow dislocations are common in children and occur when the upper arm bone (humerus) and one of the forearm bones (ulna) lose their alignment.
- **Hip dislocation** — usually caused by high-impact trauma, such as car accidents or severe falls, hip dislocations often accompany other injuries. This injury can restrict blood supply to the hip, leading to complications.
- **Knee dislocation** — occurs when the thigh bone (femur) and the shin bone (tibia) lose their alignment. Knee dislocations can damage arteries and nerves and often require surgical intervention.
- **Finger and toe dislocations** — occur in the small joints of the fingers and toes, usually from sports injuries or falls. They are common in contact sports or activities involving gripping.

### Symptoms of Dislocation
- **Pain** — severe and immediate pain at the joint, which worsens with movement.
- **Deformity** — the joint may appear visibly out of place, with an unusual shape or bump.
- **Swelling and bruising** — the area around the dislocated joint often swells and may bruise due to blood vessel damage.
- **Limited mobility** — difficulty moving the joint due to pain or misalignment.
- **Numbness or tingling** — dislocations may press on nerves, causing tingling, numbness, or weakness around the joint.

### Complications of Dislocations
- **Joint instability** — after a dislocation, some joints may become prone to recurrent dislocations, particularly if surrounding tissues have been damaged.
- **Nerve or blood vessel damage** — dislocations can injure nerves and blood vessels around the joint, potentially leading to numbness, weakness, or impaired circulation.
- **Osteoarthritis** — a dislocated joint may develop osteoarthritis over time, especially if cartilage has been damaged during the injury.
- **Avascular necrosis** — in cases where blood supply to the bone is compromised (e.g. hip dislocations), there is a risk of avascular necrosis, which is the death of bone tissue due to a lack of blood supply.

## Using a Splint
Splints are often used in emergency settings to stabilize injuries temporarily before a person can receive further medical treatment or be transported to a hospital. They can also be used as a longer-term solution for certain injuries when complete immobilization is not required.

### Functions of Splints
1. **Immobilization** — keeps the injured area from moving, preventing further injury and promoting healing.
2. **Pain reduction** — stabilizing an injured limb or joint minimizes movement, reducing pain and discomfort.
3. **Protection** — protects the injured area from external pressure, bumps, or further trauma.
4. **Swelling control** — by keeping the injured part in a fixed position, splints can help control swelling and inflammation.

### Types of Splints
- **Rigid splints** — made of firm materials like wood, plastic, or metal, rigid splints provide strong support and are often used in emergency situations.
- **Soft splints** — made from soft materials, like foam or padded fabric, providing gentle support for injuries that require some stability but don't need complete immobilization.
- **Air splints** — inflatable splints that provide uniform pressure and support. Air splints are often used for limb fractures and are popular in emergency care because they are easy to apply.
- **Traction splints** — specialized splints used primarily for femur (thigh bone) fractures. They apply a pulling force (traction) to keep the bone aligned and reduce muscle spasm.
- **Anatomical splints** — using another part of the body as a splint. For example, an injured arm can be held close to the chest and bandaged in place, or an injured leg can be tied to the other leg.

### When to Use a Splint
- **Fractures** — to immobilize broken bones and prevent further injury.
- **Sprains and strains** — to provide support and limit movement in injured joints or muscles.
- **Dislocations** — to stabilize a dislocated joint until it can be treated.
- **Post-surgical support** — after certain surgeries, splints can provide stability to aid healing.
- **Soft tissue injuries** — in some cases, splints are used to rest and support an area with ligament or tendon damage.

### Principles of Splinting
- Identify the fracture site. Stop the bleeding using bandages, but avoid pressing on the painful, deformed fracture site.
- In bone fractures where bone ends protrude through the skin, do not push these ends back in place as this will cause inflammation and acute bleeding.
- Keep the fractured bone (including the joints above and below the fracture site) motionless.
  - If the lower arm is fractured, keep the wrist and elbow joints motionless.
  - If the upper arm is fractured, keep the shoulder and elbow joints motionless.
  - If the lower leg is fractured, keep the knee and ankle joints motionless.
  - If the upper leg is fractured, keep the hip and knee joints motionless.
- The splint should be tied firmly to immobilize the fractured limb, then check for blood circulation to ensure the splinting is not too tight. Correct splinting provides pain relief.
- If the fractured limb is bent with a sharp bone end protruding through the skin, keep it motionless. Splint a limb as you find it, to make it as comfortable to the patient as possible.

### How to Apply a Splint
1. **Prepare the materials** — use a rigid material like a piece of wood, plastic, rolled-up newspaper, or another item that can provide support. Padding (cloth or gauze) should be used to avoid direct pressure on the skin.
2. **Align the limb** — without moving the injury excessively, keep the limb in a natural position. Do not try to realign any displaced bones.
3. **Apply padding** — place soft padding between the splint and the skin to prevent pressure sores and improve comfort.
4. **Place the splint** — position the splint on the injured area, ensuring it extends beyond the injury on both sides to prevent movement.
5. **Secure the splint** — use bandages, tape, or cloth strips to secure the splint in place. Ensure it's tight enough to hold the splint but not so tight as to restrict blood flow.
6. **Check for circulation** — check the skin below the splint for warmth, color, and sensation to ensure blood flow is not restricted.

## Soft Tissue Injuries
Soft tissue injuries are injuries that affect the tissues that connect, support, and surround other structures and organs in the body, including muscles, tendons, ligaments, and skin. These injuries can result from trauma, overuse, or strain and are common in sports, daily activities, and accidents. Soft tissue injuries can vary in severity from mild strains and bruises to more serious tears and sprains.

### Types
**Sprains** — an injury to a ligament, the fibrous tissue that connects bones to each other at a joint.
- Common areas for sprains include the ankle, wrist, and knee.
- Symptoms include pain, swelling, bruising, and limited joint movement.

**Strains** — an injury to a muscle or tendon (the tissue connecting muscles to bones) caused by overstretching or tearing.
- Common areas for strains include the back, hamstrings, and shoulder muscles.
- Symptoms include pain, muscle spasms, swelling, and sometimes limited mobility.

**Contusions (bruises)** — caused by direct impact or trauma, leading to blood vessel damage and bleeding under the skin.
- Symptoms include discoloration, swelling, and tenderness in the affected area.

**Tendinitis** — inflammation of a tendon, typically caused by repetitive motion or overuse, often seen in athletes or people with physically demanding jobs.
- Common sites include the elbow (tennis elbow), shoulder, wrist, and Achilles tendon.
- Symptoms include pain, tenderness, and restricted movement in the affected area.

### General Symptoms
- **Pain** — ranging from mild to severe, depending on the extent of the injury.
- **Swelling** — caused by inflammation and, in some cases, fluid buildup.
- **Bruising** — discoloration under the skin due to blood vessel damage.
- **Tenderness** — increased sensitivity and discomfort in the injured area.
- **Reduced range of motion** — difficulty moving the affected joint or muscle.
- **Weakness or instability** — feeling of weakness in the injured area, especially in joints.

### Causes
- **Trauma or direct impact** — falls, blows, or accidents can damage soft tissues.
- **Overstretching or overloading** — lifting heavy weights, sudden movements, or excessive stretching can strain muscles and ligaments.
- **Repetitive motion** — repeating the same movements over time (e.g. running, typing, throwing) can cause micro-injuries that accumulate over time.
- **Inadequate warm-up** — failing to properly warm up before physical activity can increase the risk of strains and sprains.

### First Aid (P.R.I.C.E.)
- **Protect** — protect the injured area from further damage by using support or a splint.
- **Rest** — avoid activities that could worsen the injury.
- **Ice** — apply ice for 15–20 minutes every few hours, for the first 48 hours.
- **Compression** — use a compression bandage to control swelling and provide support to the injured area.
- **Elevation** — elevate the injured area above heart level to reduce swelling.
